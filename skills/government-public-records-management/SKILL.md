# Government Public Records Management

Public records management is how a government body handles the
lifecycle of its official records — creation, retention, and eventual
disposal or permanent archiving. It exists because governments are
usually legally required to keep certain records for a defined period
(sometimes forever), and the specific retention schedules, classification
rules, and archiving requirements vary significantly by country and
even by agency within the same country, so this is general structural
guidance, not a substitute for the applicable records-retention law.

## Key components

- **A retention schedule per record category** — specifying how long
  each category of record must be kept before disposal or archiving is
  even permitted, since different record types (personnel files,
  financial records, meeting minutes, correspondence) typically carry
  different required retention periods.
- **Consistent classification at the point of creation** — records
  tagged with the correct category as soon as they're created, so the
  correct retention schedule can actually be applied to them later.
- **A defined process for permanent or historically significant
  records** — a path for records with lasting historical or legal value
  to go to an archive, distinct from the routine disposal process that
  applies to everyday records.
- **A documented disposal process with an audit trail** — records
  destroyed only once their retention period has run, with a record
  kept of what was disposed of, when, and under whose authority.
- **Access and security controls appropriate to sensitivity** — a
  record's classification often also determines who can access it and
  under what conditions, separate from how long it's kept.

## Why records must be classified correctly at the point of creation, not later

A record misfiled or left unclassified when it's created can be
destroyed on a routine schedule that never should have applied to it —
or kept indefinitely at needless storage cost and legal risk, because
no one ever assigned it a retention period at all. Both mistakes are
hard to catch after the fact: once a record is gone, there's no way to
know it should have been archived; once years have passed with no
classification, no one can be confident what its correct retention
period ever was. Classifying at creation, when the record's purpose and
context are still known, is far more reliable than trying to
reconstruct that later.

## Common pitfalls

- **Records not classified at creation** — so the wrong retention
  schedule, or none at all, gets applied later, when the context needed
  to classify them correctly has been lost.
- **Historically significant records disposed of under a routine
  schedule** — a record with real historical or legal value destroyed
  because it was never flagged for archival review, rather than
  because anyone decided it should go.
- **Retention schedules that exist on paper but aren't followed in
  daily practice** — a documented schedule that staff don't actually
  apply is no better than having no schedule at all, and creates a false
  sense that records are being managed correctly.
- **No audit trail for disposal** — records destroyed with no record of
  what was destroyed, when, or under whose authority, leaving no way to
  demonstrate the disposal was proper if it's later questioned.
- **Retention treated as a one-time setup rather than an ongoing
  process** — a schedule written once and never revisited as laws,
  agency functions, or record types change becomes outdated without
  anyone noticing.
