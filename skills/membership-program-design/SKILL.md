# Membership Program Design

A membership program converts occasional supporters into recurring
revenue by offering a defined, repeatable exchange: dues paid on a
set cadence in return for specific, consistent benefits. It differs
from major-gift fundraising in kind, not just scale — a membership
program is a product with defined tiers and renewal mechanics, not a
personalized relationship built gift by gift.

## Key components

- **Tier structure** — a small number of clearly differentiated price
  points, each with benefits that scale in a way members can see is
  worth the price difference, rather than tiers distinguished only by
  a bigger number.
- **Benefits that are actually deliverable** — every benefit promised
  at a tier has to be something the organization can reliably provide
  at the volume membership will generate, not a benefit that works at
  ten members and breaks at a thousand.
- **Renewal cadence and mechanics** — a defined renewal date, an
  automated reminder sequence, and (where the payment system supports
  it) an auto-renew default, since renewal that depends on a member
  remembering to re-subscribe manually loses members to simple
  inertia, not dissatisfaction.
- **Acquisition channel and cost** — a specific plan for how new
  members are found and what it costs to acquire one, checked against
  what a member is worth over their membership lifetime.
- **Lapsed-member win-back** — a defined process for re-engaging a
  member who didn't renew, since a lapsed member is meaningfully
  cheaper to win back than a new one is to acquire from scratch.

## Recurring revenue changes what "success" means

A one-time gift or event succeeds the moment it's received; a
membership program only succeeds if the member renews, which pushes
the real design work past the initial sale and into what happens
between renewal dates. A tier priced right and sold well but never
followed by genuine engagement — updates, access, a reason to feel
part of something — renews poorly regardless of how attractive the
initial offer was. This is why a membership program is judged on
renewal rate and lifetime value, not first-year sign-ups: a program
that acquires aggressively but retains poorly can look successful on a
first-year revenue chart while actually losing money once acquisition
cost is weighed against how few members stick around to renew.

## Common pitfalls

- **Benefits that don't scale** — a personal-touch benefit ("a call
  with the director") promised at a price point where it can't
  actually be delivered to every member who signs up at that tier.
- **No auto-renew default** — relying on members to manually
  re-subscribe loses a meaningful share to forgetting alone, not
  dissatisfaction with the program.
- **Success measured at sign-up, not renewal** — tracking first-year
  membership sales without tracking renewal rate hides whether the
  program is actually sustainable past its first cohort.
- **Tiers that don't clearly differentiate** — pricing levels
  distinguished mainly by a bigger number rather than a visibly
  different benefit set gives members no reason to choose a higher
  tier.
- **No win-back process for lapsed members** — treating a non-renewal
  as a closed case rather than a re-engagement opportunity discards a
  cheaper source of revenue than new acquisition.
