# Crisis Communication Plan

A crisis communication plan is a pre-prepared plan for how an
organization talks to the outside world — media, customers, the
public — while a crisis is unfolding. It exists so that the first
external statement isn't drafted from scratch under pressure, and so
messaging stays consistent as the situation and the facts change.

## Key components

- **A designated spokesperson** — one named person (with a backup) who
  is the only one authorized to speak externally during a crisis, so
  messaging comes from a single, consistent voice rather than several
  uncoordinated ones.
- **Holding statements prepared in advance** — draft language for
  likely scenarios, ready to adapt and release quickly rather than
  composed for the first time while the crisis is already public.
- **A communication channel plan** — which channels are used (press
  release, social media, direct customer email, a status page) and in
  what order, so the message reaches the right audiences without
  contradicting versions arriving through different channels at
  different times.
- **A process for updating the message as facts develop** — a plan for
  issuing follow-up updates on a set cadence as more is learned, rather
  than one statement followed by silence.
- **An internal notification list** — who inside the organization needs
  to know what's being said publicly, and when, so employees aren't
  learning the company's own public position from the news.
- **A correction process** — a plan for how to walk back or correct an
  earlier statement if it turns out to be wrong, without it reading as
  a cover-up.

## Why a single designated spokesperson matters

When a crisis breaks, multiple people inside an organization often feel
compelled to respond — to a reporter's call, a customer's public post,
an internal question that leaks. Left uncoordinated, this routinely
produces statements that contradict each other even when everyone is
acting in good faith, because each person knows a different slice of
an evolving situation. Those contradictions become their own story,
often making the situation look worse — more chaotic, less
trustworthy — than the underlying facts actually are. A single
designated spokesperson (with a clear backup) removes that risk:
everyone else's job during a crisis is to route inquiries to that
person, not to answer them.

## Common pitfalls

- **No prepared spokesperson** — multiple employees or executives give
  conflicting statements to media or customers because no one person
  was clearly designated to speak.
- **Silence during the information gap** — going quiet while the plan
  is being figured out reads as evasion to the outside world, even when
  it's genuine uncertainty rather than concealment; a short "we're
  aware and investigating" holding statement fills that gap.
- **A first statement that turns out wrong, with no correction plan** —
  early statements are often based on incomplete information; without
  a plan for how to credibly correct course, a wrong first statement
  becomes evidence of dishonesty rather than an honest early mistake.
- **Channels used inconsistently** — different messages going out on
  different channels at different times, so customers and media piece
  together a contradictory picture.
- **Plan never rehearsed** — the first time anyone tests the plan is
  during an actual crisis, when it's too late to notice the
  spokesperson is unreachable or the templates don't fit the situation.
- **No internal communication** — employees find out what the company
  is telling the public from outside sources instead of from leadership.
