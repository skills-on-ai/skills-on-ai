# HIIT Fitness

HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training) alternates short bursts of
near-maximal effort with brief recovery periods — a time-efficient way
to build cardiovascular fitness, popular because a relatively short
session can produce a substantial training effect.

## Structuring intervals

A HIIT session typically defines: work interval length (often seconds
to a couple of minutes), rest interval length, the number of rounds,
and the overall session length. The specific ratio of work to rest
depends on the goal — shorter, harder intervals with longer rest
develop different qualities than longer, slightly less intense
intervals with shorter rest.

## Who HIIT suits, and who should be cautious

HIIT's intensity makes it efficient but demanding — it places
significant stress on the cardiovascular system and connective
tissue. It's generally not the best starting point for complete
beginners to exercise, or for anyone with an untreated cardiovascular
condition, without first building a general fitness base and getting
appropriate medical clearance.

## Recovery demands

Because of its intensity, HIIT requires more recovery between sessions
than moderate steady-state cardio; doing HIIT every day, without
adequate rest or lower-intensity days between sessions, is a common way
to stall progress or increase injury risk rather than accelerate
fitness gains.

## Common pitfalls

- **Doing HIIT too frequently without recovery** — the intensity that
  makes HIIT effective also makes it more demanding to recover from;
  most programs schedule it only a few times per week, not daily.
- **Starting with HIIT before building a fitness base** — attempting
  high-intensity intervals without adequate baseline cardiovascular
  fitness increases both injury risk and the odds of an unpleasant,
  discouraging first experience.
- **Sacrificing form for intensity** — as fatigue builds during
  intervals, form often breaks down; this is a common source of injury
  specific to high-intensity training.
- **Treating every session as maximal effort** — pushing to true
  maximal intensity in every single interval, every session, is
  unsustainable and increases burnout and injury risk relative to a
  more measured approach.
