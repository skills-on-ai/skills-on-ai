# Thai Language

Thai (ภาษาไทย, Phasa Thai) is the official language of Thailand. It is
written in its own script without spaces between words, is tonal, and
encodes politeness through a combination of pronoun choice and
sentence-final particles that differ depending on the speaker's own
gender.

## Writing system: an abugida without word spacing

Thai script is an **abugida**: each consonant letter carries an
inherent vowel sound by default, and other vowels are shown by adding
vowel marks around the consonant (before, after, above, or below it),
rather than every vowel being a separate letter in the main line of
text the way Latin vowels are. Thai also has a genuinely distinctive
spacing convention: words within a sentence are written with **no
spaces between them**. Spaces are instead used mainly to mark clause or
sentence boundaries, similar to how a comma or period functions in
English. This differs from most Latin-script languages, where spacing
between individual words is the default, and it means word segmentation
in Thai text has to be inferred from context and vocabulary rather than
read directly off whitespace.

## Tone

Standard Thai has five tones — commonly described as mid, low, falling,
high, and rising — and, as in Mandarin and Vietnamese, the same base
syllable spoken with a different tone is a different word. Thai tone is
not shown by a single consistent set of diacritic marks the way
Vietnamese tone is. Instead, a syllable's tone is determined by a
combination of: an explicit tone mark (one of several marks placed above
the consonant) when present, the class of the initial consonant (Thai
consonants are grouped into low, mid, and high classes with different
tone behavior), and vowel length (long vs. short). This layered system
means the same visible tone mark can indicate different tones depending
on the consonant class it appears with, so tone cannot be read off a
single mark in isolation the way it often can in Vietnamese.

## No conjugation, plurals, or gender — sentence-final particles mark politeness

Thai verbs do not conjugate for tense, person, or number, nouns carry no
grammatical gender, and plurality is not marked on the noun itself
(context, numbers with classifiers, or explicit plural words carry
that meaning instead, similar in spirit to Mandarin's measure-word
system). Politeness and formality are instead marked largely by adding
**particles at the end of a sentence** — most notably ครับ (khrap),
used by male speakers, and ค่ะ/คะ (kha/ka), used by female speakers —
which soften a statement or question and signal respect. Because these
particles are tied to the speaker's own gender rather than the
listener's, a text or speech generated for a given speaker must pick
the particle matching that speaker's gender, not the audience's.

## Hierarchical pronouns and politeness

Thai has a large set of personal pronouns and self-reference terms
whose appropriate choice depends on the relative status, age, and
familiarity between speaker and listener — conceptually similar to
Korean's and Japanese's honorific systems in that getting it wrong
signals a real social misjudgment, though structured differently: Thai
does not conjugate verbs for honorific level the way Korean and Japanese
do, and instead concentrates politeness in pronoun/self-reference
choice, vocabulary register (a more elevated vocabulary layer exists for
royalty and monks specifically), and the sentence-final particles above.
Common pronouns range from neutral/polite (ผม phom for "I," male) to
intimate or hierarchical terms used only with close relations or people
of clearly different status; the fully neutral "you"/"I" that English
defaults to does not have a single unmarked Thai equivalent in the same
way.

## Common pitfalls

- Assuming Thai script has spaces between words the way Latin-script
  languages do — segmentation must be inferred from vocabulary and
  context, and spaces mark clause/sentence boundaries instead.
- Reading a tone mark alone without accounting for consonant class and
  vowel length, which determine the actual tone alongside the mark.
- Using the wrong gendered sentence-final particle (ครับ vs. ค่ะ/คะ) for
  the speaker's actual or intended gender.
- Defaulting to one "neutral" pronoun for "I" or "you" without
  considering the relative status/familiarity that Thai pronoun choice
  is expected to reflect.
- Using ordinary vocabulary where the special elevated register for
  royalty or monks is required, or vice versa, in contexts that call
  for it.
