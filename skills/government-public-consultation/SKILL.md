# Government Public Consultation

A public consultation is a structured process a government body runs
to gather public input before a decision is made, rather than after.
It's proactive, not reactive: unlike a [[freedom-of-information-request]],
which is a request for records the government already holds, a
consultation is an invitation for input on a decision that hasn't been
made yet. What triggers a consultation requirement, how long it must
run, and what form it takes vary by country and level of government —
a UK government consultation, a US federal or state public-input
process, and a local council consultation each have their own rules,
so check the applicable requirement before relying on any one model as
universal.

## Key components

- **A clearly scoped question** — a specific decision, proposal, or set
  of options the consultation is actually asking about, stated
  precisely enough that a response can be evaluated against it.
- **Genuinely accessible channels** — ways to respond that reach people
  beyond those already following the issue: not just a webpage or
  online form, but outreach through channels the affected public
  actually uses (local notices, community organizations, in-person
  sessions, non-English-language materials where relevant).
- **A defined and adequate response period** — enough time, clearly
  communicated, for people to become aware of the consultation, read
  the proposal, and respond, rather than a technically-open window too
  short or too obscure to generate meaningful participation.
- **A published summary of what was heard** — the range of views
  received, including significant disagreement, made public rather than
  held internally.
- **A stated account of how the input influenced the decision** — what
  changed as a result of what was heard, or a stated reason why it
  didn't, published alongside or shortly after the final decision.

## Why publishing what changed (or didn't) matters for credibility

A consultation whose outcome was effectively decided before it opened,
with input gathered only as a procedural formality, is recognized as
such quickly — respondents notice when a final decision bears no
visible relationship to what was said during the consultation. That
recognition doesn't stay contained to the one decision; it teaches
people that engaging with the next consultation is a waste of their
time, which lowers response quality and participation on every future
consultation that body runs. Publishing a clear account of what was
heard and how it did or didn't change the outcome is what makes the
next consultation worth responding to — it's the mechanism that keeps
the process itself credible over time, not just this one decision.

## Common pitfalls

- **A question posed so broadly that responses are unusable** — an open
  invitation to comment on anything related to a broad topic produces
  a scattered set of responses that can't actually be weighed against
  each other or used to inform a specific decision.
- **Engagement limited to channels that reach only the already-engaged**
  — publishing a consultation solely on a website or portal that only
  people already following the issue would think to check excludes the
  wider public the consultation is meant to reach, and skews the
  responses received toward existing stakeholders.
- **A response period too short or too obscure to generate real
  participation** — technically satisfying a minimum notice period
  while giving affected people little practical chance to learn about
  it and respond defeats the purpose of consulting at all.
- **A decision announced afterward with no visible connection to what
  was heard** — publishing a final decision without any account of the
  input received, or with only a token nod to "public feedback," leaves
  no way to tell whether the consultation actually mattered.
- **Treating a large but shallow response volume as if it settled the
  question** — a high number of near-identical responses (for example,
  from a campaign) can look like strong support or opposition without
  reflecting the breadth of views actually held; volume isn't a
  substitute for weighing the substance of what was said.
