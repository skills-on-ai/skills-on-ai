# Succession Planning

Succession planning is the practice of identifying which roles are
critical enough that losing the current holder would create real risk,
naming people who could plausibly step into those roles, and actively
developing them toward readiness. It's a forward-looking risk-reduction
practice, not a one-time list of names attached to a role — a plan that
stops at naming a successor without developing them is a plan in name
only.

## Key components

- **Critical-role identification** — an honest assessment of which
  roles actually warrant a succession plan because their sudden vacancy
  would materially disrupt the organization, rather than assuming every
  role needs one.
- **Potential successors, honestly assessed** — one or more candidates
  per critical role, each with a genuine readiness assessment (ready
  now, ready in one to two years, ready longer-term), not just a name
  written next to the role.
- **A development plan closing the specific gap** — concrete actions
  (stretch assignments, mentoring, training, exposure to the role's
  actual responsibilities) targeted at the specific difference between
  a successor's current readiness and what the role requires.
- **Regular review** — readiness assessments and development plans
  revisited on a set cadence, since both the roles and the candidates'
  readiness change over time.
- **Risk coverage across the organization** — critical roles identified
  wherever they actually are, not only at the most senior levels.

## Why the readiness gap matters more than the name

Writing a name next to a role answers "who would we call," but it
doesn't answer "are they actually ready, and if not, what's being done
about it." A successor who is named but never given the stretch
assignments, mentoring, or exposure that would close their specific
readiness gap is no more prepared on the day they're needed than if no
one had been named at all. The honest gap assessment — what does this
person still need in order to be ready — is what turns a list of names
into an actual plan.

## Common pitfalls

- **A successor named with no development plan** — naming someone
  without any concrete action to close their readiness gap leaves the
  organization exactly as exposed as having no plan, while creating a
  false sense of coverage.
- **Only the most senior roles covered** — succession planning focused
  solely on the CEO or executive team while other genuinely critical
  roles (a sole specialist, a key technical owner) have no plan at all
  leaves real risk unaddressed.
- **Plans kept secret from the successors themselves** — when named
  successors don't know they're being considered, they get no chance to
  seek out the development, exposure, or feedback that would actually
  prepare them, and the plan quietly fails to do its job.
- **Readiness assessed once and never revisited** — a candidate judged
  "ready in two years" three years ago, with no update since, may no
  longer be the right assessment in either direction.
- **Single point of failure per role** — naming only one successor with
  no backup means the plan itself has no redundancy if that person also
  leaves or turns out not to be interested.
