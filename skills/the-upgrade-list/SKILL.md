# The Upgrade List

Some things are used every day and quietly annoy: the thin mattress
topper, the flimsy kettle, the pan that sticks, the laptop bag that
digs in. The upgrade list is a running record of those annoyances,
ranked by how often the item is used and how much it bothers, so that
spending goes where daily use is highest and impulse buys lose. Items
are bought one at a time, when the case is clear.

## Input required

- **Candidate items**: things used often that work poorly, wear out, or
  cause friction.
- **Frequency of use** for each, in uses per day or week.
- **What is wrong** with the current one, specifically.
- **Budget**, any savings set aside for this, and any spending limits.
- **Country and context**, since availability, warranty, and repair
  options vary.

## Questions to ask first

Ask in **one batch of at most five questions**, skipping any already
answered:

1. Which items do you use every day that bother you or are close to
   failing?
2. For each, what exactly is wrong, and how often do you use it?
3. How much can you spend per month on upgrades without strain?
4. Is the current item repairable, or would a cheaper fix solve it?
5. Do you tend to buy on impulse, and if so, in what category?

## Method

1. **Collect candidates.** Keep a note on the phone. When something
   annoys, add it with one line on what went wrong. Collect for a week
   or two before ranking.
2. **Score each item.** Use three simple measures:
   - *Use*: how many times per week.
   - *Pain*: how much it costs in comfort, time, or safety.
   - *Fix cost*: roughly what a better version costs.
   Rank by use times pain, then weigh against cost. Rough scores on a
   1 to 5 scale are enough; do not pretend to precision.
3. **Try cheaper fixes first.** Repair, clean, adjust, or change how
   it is used. Many annoyances are a missing small part, not a bad
   item.
4. **Define what "better" means.** Write two or three criteria:
   durability, comfort, repairability, size, noise, or safety. Without
   criteria the upgrade can be a different annoyance.
5. **Research once, calmly.** Read independent reviews and warranty
   terms, and check whether spare parts exist. Treat reviews as input,
   not proof. Name no brand unless the user asks; describe what to look
   for.
6. **Apply a waiting period.** Leave the item on the list for a set
   time before buying, such as two weeks as a default. If it still
   ranks near the top, proceed.
7. **Buy one at a time.** Pay from the earmarked amount. Where a
   second-hand or refurbished option works, consider it.
8. **Retire the old item.** Sell, donate, recycle, or keep as a
   deliberate spare. Do not let the old one sit in a drawer.
9. **Review the list.** Monthly, drop items that stopped bothering the
   user, and re-rank. Record, after a month of use, whether the
   upgrade was worth it. That record improves future judgement.

### Quick test for any purchase

| Question | If the answer is no |
|---|---|
| Will I use it at least weekly? | Reconsider |
| Does it fix a specific pain I can name? | Wait |
| Is a repair or cheaper fix possible? | Try that first |
| Have I waited out the delay? | Wait |

## Output format

- **Ranked list**: item, use, pain, fix cost, rank.
- **Criteria** for the top three items.
- **Cheaper-fix notes** where relevant.
- **Purchase order** with the waiting date and earmarked amount.
- **Review log template**: bought, date, satisfied or not, why.

## Ground rules

- **No endorsements.** Describe qualities to look for; the user picks
  the product. Do not state prices, features, or plan details.
- **Money limits first.** Upgrades do not come from debt or from money
  needed for essentials. Point to [[personal-budget]] where unsure.
- **Do not encourage constant dissatisfaction.** The list is for things
  used daily that really are worse than they should be.
- **Never ask for** card, account, or ID numbers.
- **Safety items** (smoke alarms, tyres, helmets) go to the top when
  worn or expired; tell the user to follow the maker's guidance.

## Self-check before answering

- Are items ranked by use and pain, not by desire?
- Was a cheaper fix considered?
- Are there written criteria and a waiting period?
- Is each purchase paid from an agreed amount?
- Is there a plan for the old item?

## Common pitfalls

- **Upgrading rarely used things** because they are interesting.
- **Buying the trending thing** instead of the criteria.
- **Never reviewing**: the same bad choices repeat.
- **Replacing too many things at once**, spending the whole budget.
- **Keeping the old item** as clutter.
- **Confusing upgrade with hobby spending**, which is fine but separate.

## Example prompt

> Here are twelve things I use daily that annoy me: my desk chair,
> my headphones, my kettle, my backpack. Rank them, tell me which to
> try fixing cheaply, set criteria for the top three, and give me an
> order to buy them over a year.
