# Strength Training Fitness

Strength training is exercise that builds muscular strength and size
by working against resistance — free weights, machines, resistance
bands, or bodyweight — typically organized as a program of sets and
reps performed on a regular schedule.

## Progressive overload is the core principle

Strength gains come from progressively increasing the demand placed on
a muscle over time — more weight, more reps, more sets, or less rest
— not from any single exercise or program. A program that never
increases demand eventually plateaus, regardless of how consistently
it's followed; tracking workouts is what makes progressive overload
possible to apply deliberately rather than by accident.

## Compound lifts as a foundation

Compound exercises (squats, deadlifts, presses, rows) work multiple
muscle groups and joints at once, producing more strength and
efficiency per workout than isolation exercises (which target a single
muscle). Building a program around a handful of compound lifts, with
isolation work added around them, is a common and effective structure
for most goals.

## Program structure basics

- **Frequency** — how often a muscle group is trained per week.
- **Volume** — total sets and reps performed, the main driver of
  muscle growth (hypertrophy) once intensity is adequate.
- **Intensity** — how heavy the weight is relative to what could be
  lifted for one rep (a 1-rep max), which drives strength adaptations
  most directly at higher intensities.

Beginners generally progress well on relatively simple programs with
consistent compound lifts; more complex periodization schemes become
more valuable as a lifter approaches their genetic and training-age
limits.

## Common pitfalls

- **Poor form to lift heavier weight** — sacrificing technique for
  load is one of the most common causes of injury, and often means the
  weight isn't even training the intended muscles effectively.
- **Programs with no progressive overload** — repeating the exact
  same weights and reps indefinitely produces a plateau; some form of
  deliberate progression is necessary for continued gains.
- **Neglecting recovery** — training the same muscle group without
  adequate rest between sessions can impair progress rather than
  accelerate it; see [[recovery-and-rest-fitness]].
- **Program-hopping too frequently** — switching programs every few
  weeks, before giving one enough time to produce results, makes it
  hard to tell what's actually working.
