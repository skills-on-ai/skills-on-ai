# Gaeilge Language

Gaeilge (Irish) is a Celtic language and an official language of both
Ireland and the European Union. It is spoken as a first language by a
minority, concentrated in Gaeltacht regions, but is studied and used
widely across Ireland in education, government, media, and public
signage.

## Writing system

Irish uses the Latin alphabet with the síneadh fada (an acute accent,
e.g. á, é, í, ó, ú) marking long vowels. The fada is not decorative —
omitting it can change a word's meaning entirely, e.g. `sean` (old) vs.
`séan` (denial/omen), or `ar` (on) vs. `ár` (our). Never strip fadas
when normalizing or transliterating Irish text, and never treat a word
missing its fada as equivalent to the correctly accented form.

## Initial mutations

Like Welsh, Irish changes the beginning of a word depending on
grammatical context, though the specific rules and mutation names
differ:

- **Lenition (séimhiú)** — adds an "h" after certain initial consonants,
  softening the sound, triggered after many possessive pronouns,
  particles, and prepositions. Example: `bád` (boat) becomes `bhád`
  after "mo" (my): `mo bhád`.
- **Eclipsis (urú)** — replaces or prefixes the initial sound with
  another, triggered after certain prepositions, numbers, and the
  plural possessive. Example: `carr` (car) becomes `gcarr` after "a"
  (their): `a gcarr`.

As with Welsh, a mutated word must still be recognized and looked up
under its unmutated root form, and applying no mutation at all is a
common and noticeable error.

## Word order

Irish uses verb-subject-object (VSO) word order, unlike English's
subject-verb-object: "Tá mé ag obair" (Am I at-work / I am working)
places the verb first. This affects how sentences should be
constructed or parsed when translating to or from English, and machine
translation that defaults to English-like SVO ordering will produce
ungrammatical Irish.

## Regional variation

Irish has three main dialect groups — Ulster (Ulaidh), Connacht
(Connacht), and Munster (Mumhan) — with real differences in vocabulary,
idiom, and pronunciation, not just accent. Alongside these, the
Caighdeán Oifigiúil (the official standard) is the standardized written
form used in official documents, education, and most published text.
Default to the Caighdeán for formal or general-purpose writing; use a
specific dialect only when the audience or context (e.g. representing a
speaker from a specific Gaeltacht region) calls for it.

## Formality/register

Irish has formal and informal address patterns reflected mainly in
vocabulary and politeness conventions rather than a rigid tu/vous verb
system; written Irish in official, educational, and media contexts
tends toward the Caighdeán's fuller forms, while conversational and
Gaeltacht-dialect speech uses more contracted and locally distinctive
forms.

## Common pitfalls

- Dropping fadas (síntí fada) when writing or normalizing Irish text —
  this changes word meanings, not just appearance, and is a common
  error when text passes through systems not designed for accented
  Latin scripts.
- Applying no initial mutation, or applying the wrong mutation type
  (lenition where eclipsis is required, or vice versa) for the
  triggering word.
- Defaulting to English SVO word order instead of Irish's VSO order.
- Mixing dialect-specific vocabulary from Ulster, Connacht, and Munster
  inconsistently within one document instead of using the Caighdeán
  Oifigiúil or committing to one dialect.
- Treating Gaeilge and Gàidhlig (Scottish Gaelic) as the same language
  or as interchangeable — they are closely related but distinct
  languages with limited mutual intelligibility in writing; see
  [[gaidhlig-language]].
