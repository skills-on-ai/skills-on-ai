# Cardio Fitness

Cardiovascular (cardio) fitness training improves the heart and lungs'
capacity to deliver oxygen to working muscles — through activities
like running, cycling, swimming, or rowing — and is a distinct
training goal from strength or flexibility, though most well-rounded
programs include all three.

## Heart-rate zones

Cardio training is often organized around heart-rate zones, roughly
from very light effort up to maximal effort. Most training benefit for
general fitness comes from spending the bulk of training time at
lower-to-moderate intensities (building an aerobic base), with a
smaller portion at high intensity — not from constantly training at
maximal effort, which is both unsustainable and, for most goals, less
effective than a mixed approach.

## Steady-state vs. interval training

- **Steady-state cardio** — sustained effort at a consistent, moderate
  intensity for an extended period; builds aerobic base efficiently and
  is generally lower-impact and easier to sustain regularly.
- **Interval training** — alternating short bursts of high intensity
  with recovery periods; produces significant fitness benefits in less
  total time, but is more demanding and generally needs more recovery
  between sessions (see [[hiit-fitness]] for a specific structured
  approach).

## Building an aerobic base

A large aerobic base (built mostly through lower-intensity, sustained
training) supports better performance and faster recovery across
nearly all other fitness goals, including strength training and
interval work — it's often underrated relative to higher-intensity
training, especially by beginners eager to see fast results.

## Common pitfalls

- **Training too intensely, too often** — spending most training time
  at high intensity, rather than building a base at lower intensities,
  is a common cause of burnout, injury, and stalled progress.
- **Ignoring heart rate/effort feedback** — training purely by a fixed
  pace or speed target, regardless of how the body is actually
  responding on a given day, can push into overtraining.
- **Progressing duration or intensity too quickly** — similar to
  strength training, cardio fitness improves through gradual, consistent
  progression, not large jumps.
- **Neglecting variety** — relying on a single cardio activity
  exclusively can lead to overuse injury from repetitive movement
  patterns; mixing modes (running, cycling, swimming) reduces this risk.
