# Scots Language

Scots is a Germanic language spoken in Lowland Scotland and, as Ulster
Scots, in parts of Northern Ireland. It is a distinct Anglic language,
not a Celtic one — it is unrelated in origin to Scottish Gaelic
([[gaidhlig-language]]), which belongs to the entirely separate Celtic
branch. Scots and English are closely related (both descend from Old
English), which makes it easy to mistake Scots for a mere accent or
dialect of English, but it developed separately over centuries with its
own core vocabulary, grammar, and literary tradition (Robert Burns being
its best-known literary figure).

## Scots as its own language, not "English with an accent"

Treat Scots as a genuinely distinct language rather than English
respelled to sound colloquial. Evidence for this distinctness:

- **Distinct core vocabulary** not derived from English — words like
  "bairn" (child), "wee" (small), "ken" (know), "dinnae" (don't), and
  "aye" (yes) are native Scots vocabulary, not English slang.
- **Separate historical development** — Scots and English both evolved
  from Old English/Northumbrian roots but diverged over centuries as
  separate branches of the Anglic language family, with Scots
  developing under different political and literary influences.
- **Its own literary tradition** — a substantial body of poetry and
  prose (Burns, and a continuous tradition before and after him)
  written in Scots as a language in its own right, not as English
  dialogue rendered phonetically for local color.

Grammar and idiom differ genuinely from English too, not just
vocabulary: verb forms, negation patterns ("I dinnae ken" rather than "I
don't know"), and idiomatic constructions follow Scots' own patterns.

## Spelling conventions

Unlike a language with one official orthography, Scots has no single,
fully standardized spelling system — multiple spelling conventions
coexist (varying, for instance, in how they render vowel sounds or
whether they use apostrophes to mark "missing" English letters, e.g.
"wi'" vs. "wi" for "with"). Because of this, internal consistency
within a document matters more than matching one "correct" spelling:
pick one convention (or follow the source text's existing convention
when editing) and apply it uniformly, rather than mixing spellings from
different traditions or "correcting" one valid spelling to another.

## Regional variation

Scots varies regionally — Doric (North-East Scotland), Glaswegian, and
Ulster Scots (Northern Ireland) among others — with real differences in
vocabulary and idiom. Ulster Scots in particular has its own distinct
institutional recognition and standardization efforts in Northern
Ireland, separate from Scots as spoken in Scotland.

## Common pitfalls

- **Defaulting to "English with dialect words swapped in"** — the most
  common failure mode for an AI system: substituting a handful of Scots
  vocabulary items (wee, bairn, aye) into otherwise standard English
  sentence structure, rather than genuinely following Scots grammar and
  idiom.
- **"Correcting" a valid Scots spelling to match English** or to match a
  different Scots spelling convention than the one the document already
  uses — inconsistency introduced this way looks like an error even
  when each individual spelling is independently valid.
- **Assuming one universally "correct" spelling exists** and rejecting
  other legitimate conventions as mistakes.
- **Confusing Scots with Scottish Gaelic** ([[gaidhlig-language]]) —
  they are entirely unrelated language families (Germanic vs. Celtic),
  not variants of one language.
- **Treating Scots as unwritten or purely oral** — it has an active
  literary and journalistic tradition and should be treated as a
  written language capable of formal registers, not only informal
  speech transcription.
