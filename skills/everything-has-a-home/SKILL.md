# Everything Has a Home

The rule is simple: every belonging has one fixed place, and putting it
back takes no thought. Mess is usually not laziness. It is items with no
assigned place, or a place too inconvenient to use. This skill helps a
user assign homes, set up the few spots where things land temporarily,
and keep the total amount of stuff within the space.

## Input required

- **Which area to start with**: one room, drawer, or category, not the
  whole house.
- **Space available**: shelves, cupboards, hooks, boxes, and what is
  already in them.
- **Who shares the space** and how they use it.
- **What is lost or misplaced most often.**
- **Constraints**: renting, small space, children, mobility, budget.

## Questions to ask first

Ask in **one batch of at most five questions**, skipping any already
answered:

1. Which area or category bothers you most right now?
2. What do you lose, or keep finding in the wrong place?
3. Who else uses the space, and where do they naturally put things?
4. How much storage do you have, and how full is it?
5. How much time can you give the first pass, and can you donate or
   discard items freely?

## Method

1. **Pick a small start.** One drawer, shelf, or category. Finish it
   before moving on; a house-wide start usually stalls.
2. **Gather and sort.** Take everything out. Sort into keep, give
   away or sell, recycle or discard, and "not sure". Put the "not sure"
   items in a labeled box with a review date a few months out. If
   unopened at that date, let them go.
3. **Group like with like.** Items that serve the same purpose share a
   home: all batteries, all chargers, all tape and glue.
4. **Choose each home by use, not by tidiness.**
   - Place things where they are first needed, not where they look best.
   - The most used items go at hand height and at the front.
   - Rarely used items go high, low, or at the back.
   - Reduce steps: if putting away takes more than one or two
     motions, it will not happen. Open shelves and hooks beat lids
     and stacks for daily items.
5. **Label sparingly.** Label shared spaces and anything that others
   must find. Skip labels where the place is obvious.
6. **Create drop zones on purpose.** Keys, wallet, bag, post, and
   outgoing items need one spot near the door. Give each a hook, tray,
   or basket, and keep it small enough that overflow is visible.
   A drop zone is a temporary home, emptied on a schedule.
7. **Set the capacity limit.** A home is only a home if it has room.
   When a shelf or drawer is full, the limit applies: choose
   *one in, one out*, or *replace, do not add*. Apply it to categories
   that grow fast, such as clothes, mugs, books, and cables.
8. **Handle the awkward items.** Sentimental things get a single box
   with a size limit. Paperwork goes to a small tray plus an
   [[adulting-day]] list. Gifts and hand-me-downs get a decision
   time, not a permanent perch.
9. **Teach the system.** Show everyone the new homes. Where someone
   keeps misplacing things, ask why before blaming: the home may be
   wrong.
10. **Adjust after a week.** Items that keep ending up elsewhere are
    telling you where their home should be.

## Output format

- **Home map**: a table of item or category, home location, and
  notes for the area in scope.
- **Drop zone design**: items, container, location, and emptying day.
- **Capacity rules**: category, limit, and what leaves when something
  arrives.
- **Buy list**, only if a home needs a container, kept short.
- **One-week adjustment prompt.**

## Ground rules

- **Do not push users to discard items** they are not ready to part
  with. Use the "not sure" box and a review date.
- **Heavy accumulation is not a tidiness problem.** If someone finds it
  very distressing to let go of things or the home is unsafe, suggest
  talking to a clinician or a support service.
- **Buying containers last.** Sort and reduce first; containers can
  hide the problem.
- **Respect shared space.** Do not reassign another person's things.
- **Do not claim a method belongs to any named person or brand.**

## Self-check before answering

- Does every item in scope have exactly one home?
- Is each home where the item is used?
- Is there a capacity limit and a one-in-one-out rule where needed?
- Are drop zones small and scheduled to be emptied?
- Did I avoid selling storage products?

## Common pitfalls

- **Beautiful but inconvenient homes** that nobody uses.
- **Containers before decisions**: neat boxes of things never used.
- **Drop zone becomes a dump**: it has no emptying day.
- **"Just in case" accumulation** without a review date.
- **Homes that require moving something else first.**
- **Doing it all in a weekend** and running out of energy halfway.

## Example prompt

> I can never find my keys, chargers, or the scissors, and the hall
> table is a pile. Help me give everything in the entry and kitchen
> drawers a fixed home, set up a drop zone, and make a one-in-one-out
> rule for the stuff that keeps multiplying.
