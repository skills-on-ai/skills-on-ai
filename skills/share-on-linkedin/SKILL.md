# Share on LinkedIn

LinkedIn's feed algorithm rewards dwell time and early engagement —
comments in the first hour matter more than the eventual like count —
and it truncates every post behind a "…more" link after roughly the
first 140-210 characters (about the first two to three lines). That
truncation point is the entire game: almost everyone decides whether
to expand a post based on those first couple of lines alone, so
whatever isn't in them doesn't get read.

## What works on LinkedIn

- **The hook has to survive truncation on its own.** Write the first
  line assuming it's the only line anyone sees, since for most readers
  scrolling past, it is.
- **Short paragraphs and white space, not blocks of text.** One or two
  sentences per line, blank lines between thoughts — dense paragraphs
  get skipped even after someone clicks "…more."
- **Native text (and native documents/carousels) outperform link
  posts.** The algorithm has historically favored posts that keep
  people on LinkedIn; if a link is essential, put it in the first
  comment rather than the post body, and say "link in comments" in the
  post itself.
- **Comments count more than likes.** A post that asks something
  specific enough to generate real comments — not "thoughts?" — gets
  materially more distribution than one that only collects likes.
- **Personal narrative outperforms an achievement announcement.** "Here's
  what I got wrong building this" or "here's the problem that made me
  start this" reads as content; "Excited to announce…" reads as an ad
  and gets scrolled past by an audience that sees a lot of those.
- **A handful of relevant hashtags at the end**, not scattered through
  the text — three to five is typical, more reads as spam.

## Hashtags

Hashtags on LinkedIn work mainly as a subscription/browse mechanism —
people follow specific tags and see tagged posts in a dedicated feed —
rather than as a search-ranking signal for the main feed. Pick three
to five tags that are actually specific to the project's topic (e.g. a
named discipline or niche practice area) rather than huge generic ones
like #business or #technology with millions of followers; a smaller,
targeted tag with a genuinely interested audience reaches more of the
right people than a giant one a post will be buried in. Place them as
a single line at the very end, after the ask, not woven into
sentences, and don't exceed about five — more starts to read as
keyword-stuffing to a professional audience.

## Structure for a post up to 3000 characters

Room to write more doesn't mean it should be long — most well-performing
announcement posts run 150-400 words even with a 3000-character ceiling.

1. **First line — the hook, self-contained.** The actual news or the
   actual problem, stated plainly enough to work with nothing after it
   for a reader who never expands the post.
2. **A short personal paragraph.** Why this exists, what it took, or
   what's still uncertain about it — the detail that makes it a person
   writing, not a brand.
3. **What it actually is**, in plain terms a non-specialist
   professional can follow without extra context.
4. **One specific ask.** A particular kind of feedback, an
   introduction, someone who'd find it useful — not a generic
   "thoughts welcome."
5. **An explicit, modest invitation to share**, if it would help — said
   plainly rather than instructed.
6. **Link in the first comment** (noted in the post body), plus three
   to five relevant hashtags.

## Key components

- **A hook that works with zero context**, since it has to carry the
  whole post for anyone who doesn't click through.
- **Humility over hype** — naming what's uncertain or what went wrong
  along the way reads as more credible to this audience than a
  polished win narrative, and invites help rather than just applause.
- **One concrete, answerable ask** — specific enough that a busy reader
  can act on it in the time it takes to leave a comment.
- **Line breaks doing real work** — short lines aren't a stylistic
  affectation here, they're what makes a post survive being skimmed on
  a phone.

## Common pitfalls

- **A hook that's a table-setter, not the news** — "I've been thinking
  a lot about X lately..." spends the only characters that matter
  before getting to the point.
- **A link in the post body**, which the algorithm has historically
  treated as an exit intent and suppressed accordingly.
- **Wall-of-text formatting** with no line breaks, which loses most
  readers even after they expand the post.
- **A vague closing ask** ("let me know what you think!") that produces
  likes but few comments, which is what the algorithm actually rewards.
- **Over-claiming or announcement-voice phrasing** ("thrilled to
  share," "game-changing") that reads as marketing to a professional
  audience skeptical of it.
