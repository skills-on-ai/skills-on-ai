# Instructional Materials Selection

Instructional materials selection is the process of evaluating and
choosing the textbooks, print resources, media, and other materials a
course or curriculum will use, against the objectives the curriculum
already defines. It covers any material — print or digital — while
[[classroom-technology-integration]] covers the narrower, ongoing
decision of when and how to use digital tools specifically once
they're already in the mix.

## Key components

- **Objective fit** — whether the material actually supports the
  learning objectives already defined in [[curriculum-design]], rather
  than being adopted because it's popular, familiar, or bundled with
  something else the school already bought.
- **Reading level and accessibility** — whether the material's
  complexity matches the actual students who'll use it, and whether it
  supports learners needing accommodations (large print, audio,
  translated versions, screen-reader compatibility).
- **Bias and representation review** — whether the material presents
  content fairly and represents students' backgrounds and experiences,
  reviewed deliberately rather than assumed from a publisher's
  reputation.
- **Cost and total cost of ownership** — not just sticker price, but
  replacement cost, licensing terms for digital materials, and how
  long the material remains usable before requiring a new edition or
  renewed license.
- **Adoption process** — who evaluates candidates, using what criteria,
  and who has final approval authority — often a committee and a
  formal review cycle rather than one teacher's individual choice.

## Piloting before committing

The most reliable way to catch a mismatch between a material and its
actual classroom use is to pilot it with a real group of students
before committing to a full adoption — a textbook can look excellent
in a publisher's sample chapter and still fail in practice because the
pacing doesn't match a real term, the reading level is off for the
actual students who'll use it, or the supplementary resources assumed
by the publisher (a companion website, a testing platform) don't
actually work in the school's environment. A pilot surfaces problems a
committee review of the material alone won't: whether students can
navigate it independently, whether the material holds up across a full
unit rather than just a sample chapter, and whether teachers can
actually teach from it without extensive workarounds.

Piloting has a cost — it takes a term, and it means some students
experience material that doesn't end up getting adopted — so it's
worth reserving for adoptions that are expensive, long-lived, or
difficult to reverse (a core textbook, a multi-year platform license),
rather than every supplementary resource a teacher wants to bring in.

## Common pitfalls

- **Adopted for reputation, not fit** — choosing a widely used or
  well-marketed material without checking it against this course's
  specific objectives and students.
- **No accessibility review** — discovering after adoption that a
  material has no accommodation path for students who need one, when
  that should have been checked before purchase.
- **Total cost hidden in licensing terms** — a low sticker price that
  turns out to require an annual per-student license, making the real
  cost far higher than it first appeared.
- **One person's choice presented as a committee decision** — skipping
  the actual adoption process while claiming institutional review,
  which surfaces later when the material turns out not to have been
  vetted.
- **No pilot before a large, hard-to-reverse purchase** — committing to
  a multi-year platform or core textbook based on a sample chapter
  alone, without testing it against a real class first.
