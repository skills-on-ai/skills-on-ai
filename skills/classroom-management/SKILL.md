# Classroom Management

Classroom management is the set of routines, procedures, rules, and
behavior systems that let a class run predictably without the teacher
re-negotiating expectations every session. It's infrastructure for the
whole group — how materials get distributed, how transitions happen,
how attention gets called, what the consequences are for common
disruptions — not a response to any one student's behavior.

## Key components

- **Procedures for recurring moments** — entering the room, turning in
  work, transitioning between activities, getting help, and leaving,
  each with a specific, taught sequence rather than an assumed one.
- **Rules and expectations** — a short, specific, positively-stated
  set of behavioral expectations, taught and practiced like content
  rather than posted and assumed to be understood.
- **Attention signal** — a consistent, practiced way to get the whole
  class's attention (call-and-response, raised hand, chime) that
  doesn't depend on raising the teacher's voice.
- **Consequence and reinforcement system** — a predictable, graduated
  response to both expected and unexpected behavior, known in advance
  rather than improvised in the moment.
- **Physical and material logistics** — where supplies live, how they
  move, and how the room's layout supports the routines rather than
  fighting them; see [[classroom-seating-chart]] for the seating layer
  specifically.

## Teaching procedures like content

A procedure that's only announced, not taught, will not reliably
happen — students forget, drift, or never fully understood the
sequence in the first place. Reliable classroom management treats
each recurring procedure the way a lesson treats content: model it
explicitly, have students practice it, and correct drift the same way
a wrong answer gets corrected, calmly and immediately, rather than
letting it compound over weeks. This front-loaded cost, usually heaviest
in the first two weeks of a term, is what buys back instructional time
for the rest of the year — a class that has to be told how to pass
in papers every single day is losing minutes it will never get back.
The same logic applies after any break or disruption: routines decay
without use, and re-teaching briefly after a long weekend or a
substitute stretch is cheaper than letting the drift continue
uncorrected.

## Common pitfalls

- **Rules without procedures** — posting behavioral expectations
  ("be respectful") without teaching the specific, concrete procedures
  that make them real in a given moment leaves students knowing the
  goal but not the steps.
- **Reacting instead of practicing** — treating a botched transition
  or routine as a one-off nuisance rather than a sign the procedure
  needs to be re-taught and re-practiced lets the same friction repeat
  indefinitely.
- **Inconsistent enforcement** — applying consequences differently
  session to session, or student to student, erodes the predictability
  that makes a management system work at all.
- **No attention signal** — relying on raising your voice to regain
  the room, rather than a practiced, consistent signal, trains
  students to wait for volume rather than the cue.
- **Confusing group systems with individual intervention** — treating
  one student's persistent, distinct behavior pattern as a
  classroom-management problem to solve with tighter group rules,
  when it actually needs a targeted [[student-behavior-intervention-plan]].
