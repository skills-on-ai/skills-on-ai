# Indonesian Language

Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) is the official language of Indonesia,
based on Malay and standardized separately from it. It is largely
mutually intelligible with Malaysian Malay in everyday use, despite
standardized differences in spelling, vocabulary, and some grammar
between the two national standards — treat them as closely related but
distinct target varieties rather than interchangeable.

## Writing system

Indonesian is written in a Latin-based alphabet with straightforward,
largely phonetic spelling — letters generally correspond consistently
to sounds, without the silent letters, irregular spellings, or
multiple pronunciations of the same letter combination that make
English spelling unpredictable. This makes Indonesian comparatively
easy to read aloud correctly from spelling alone, and comparatively
easy to spell correctly from pronunciation, once the (small) set of
sound-to-letter correspondences is known.

## No grammatical gender, verb conjugation, or inflected plurals

Indonesian nouns have no grammatical gender, verbs do not conjugate
for person, number, or tense, and nouns do not take a plural suffix
the way English adds "-s." Instead:
- Tense and aspect are conveyed through separate time words and
  context (e.g. sudah "already," akan "will," sedang "in the process
  of") rather than by changing the verb's form.
- Plurality is often conveyed through reduplication — repeating the
  word — rather than an inflectional ending: orang-orang means
  "people" (plural), from orang ("person"); buku-buku means "books"
  from buku ("book"). Plurality is also frequently left unmarked and
  inferred from context or a number/quantifier, since reduplication is
  not obligatory every time plurality is meant.

This makes Indonesian verbs and nouns look deceptively simple compared
to English, but it shifts the real grammatical work onto word order,
context, and separate marker words, which a translation needs to
supply explicitly since Indonesian won't encode them morphologically
the way English does.

## Affixation

Beyond reduplication, Indonesian has a rich, productive system of
prefixes and suffixes attached to root words that change a word's part
of speech or shift its meaning — for example the prefixes me-, di-,
ber-, and pe-, and suffixes like -kan and -an, combine with roots to
form verbs, nouns, or adjectives with related but distinct meanings
(e.g. ajar "to teach/learn" as a root, belajar "to study," mengajar
"to teach," pelajaran "lesson," pelajar "student"). This affixation
system is central to how new and derived words are formed, comparable
in importance to Arabic's root-and-pattern system or Turkish's
suffixation, though Indonesian's affixes attach to a root that itself
still functions as an independent, often unaffixed word.

## Formality and politeness

Indonesian formality and politeness are carried mainly through
vocabulary choice and pronoun selection rather than through verb
conjugation (which doesn't exist). Several words exist for "I" and
"you" that vary by formality, age, and relationship: saya is a
neutral/formal "I," aku is informal/intimate; Anda is a polite,
somewhat formal "you" often used in writing and with strangers, kamu
is informal "you" used with peers and people younger or close to the
speaker, and status/kinship terms (Bapak/Pak "sir/Mr.," Ibu/Bu
"ma'am/Mrs.") are frequently used in place of "you" entirely as a mark
of respect, especially toward someone older or in a professional
context. Choosing the wrong pronoun or term of address is the main way
formality goes wrong, since there's no verb form to separately signal
respect.

## Common pitfalls

- Adding an English-style plural marker instead of using reduplication
  or simply leaving plurality unmarked and inferred from context.
- Inventing a tense-marked verb form — Indonesian verbs don't conjugate
  for tense, so time must be supplied by separate words or left to
  context, not encoded on the verb.
- Using aku/kamu in a formal or unfamiliar context where saya/Anda or a
  respectful title (Bapak/Ibu) would be expected, or the reverse in
  casual contexts.
- Treating Indonesian and Malaysian Malay as fully interchangeable —
  they diverge in spelling, vocabulary, and some idioms despite being
  closely related and largely mutually intelligible.
- Assuming every plural noun in a translation needs its reduplicated
  form — reduplication is common but not obligatory, and overusing it
  can read as unnatural.
- Missing that affixation changes part of speech, not just nuance —
  swapping a prefix (e.g. me- vs. di-) can change a sentence from
  active to passive voice.
