# Freedom Of Information Request

A freedom of information request is a formal ask, addressed to a
government body, for access to official records or information it
holds. It exists because most democracies give the public a legal
right to see how government works, subject to specific exemptions —
but the exact law, the agency's process, and what's exempt differ by
country and often by agency within the same country, so this is
general guidance, not a substitute for checking the applicable law.

## Key components

- **A specific, well-scoped description of the records requested** —
  the single biggest factor in whether a request succeeds quickly.
  Naming a specific document, decision, date range, and department
  gets a fast, complete answer; a vague ask for "everything relating
  to X" does not.
- **The relevant time period** — bounding the request to the period
  that actually matters, rather than leaving it open-ended.
- **The requester's contact information** — name and a way to reach
  them, since most regimes require this to process a request (some
  allow anonymous or third-party requests; check the local rule).
- **Awareness of likely exemptions** — categories commonly withheld
  include ongoing law-enforcement investigations, personal privacy,
  national security, and internal deliberative communications, though
  the exact list and how narrowly each is applied varies by
  jurisdiction.
- **A stated format preference** — electronic copies, paper, or
  in-person inspection, if the regime allows the requester to specify
  this.

## Why over-broad requests routinely fail or get delayed

A body asked for "all records relating to X" spanning several years
can legitimately take months to respond, charge substantial search or
copying fees, or reject the request outright as unduly burdensome —
even when every individual record would otherwise be releasable. A
narrow request — a specific report, a specific meeting's minutes, a
specific decision memo from a bounded date range — can often be
answered in the statutory minimum time. Scoping tightly isn't just
politeness; it's usually the difference between an answer in weeks and
one that never comes, or comes so heavily redacted or delayed that it's
no longer useful.

## Common pitfalls

- **Request scoped so broadly it gets rejected or delayed for months**
  — "all emails mentioning X" across years of correspondence invites a
  burden objection that a request for a specific set of emails, from a
  specific sender, in a specific week, would have avoided.
- **No awareness of the jurisdiction's specific exemptions** — asking
  for a category that's obviously exempt (an open investigation's
  file, a named individual's personnel record) without narrowing
  around it stalls the entire request instead of yielding the
  releasable parts.
- **Follow-up and appeal rights not exercised** — nearly every regime
  gives the requester some right to appeal a denial, challenge
  excessive delay, or ask for a fee waiver; letting a wrongful denial
  or a blown deadline go unchallenged forfeits a remedy that was
  actually available.
- **No record kept of the request and its deadline** — without a
  tracked submission date and the statutory response window for that
  jurisdiction, it's hard to know when a delay has actually become
  actionable.
- **Assuming one country's process applies elsewhere** — FOIA
  procedures, timelines, and exemptions in the US do not transfer
  directly to the UK's FOI Act, the EU's access-to-documents regime, or
  any other country's law; each has its own rules and appeal body.
