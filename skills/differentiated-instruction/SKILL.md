# Differentiated Instruction

Differentiated instruction is adapting how a lesson is taught — its
content, the process students use to engage with it, the product they
produce, or the learning environment itself — to fit the range of
readiness, interest, and learning profile actually present in a class.
It's an informal, teacher-driven adjustment applied within ordinary
lesson planning, not a formal legal document written and monitored for
one specific student.

## Key components

- **Content differentiation** — varying what students engage with: the
  complexity of a text, the depth of a topic, or the entry point into
  a concept, while keeping the underlying objective the same for all.
- **Process differentiation** — varying how students work toward the
  objective: independent versus scaffolded practice, pace, or grouping,
  matched to readiness rather than applied uniformly.
- **Product differentiation** — varying how students demonstrate
  learning: a written response, a presentation, a diagram, or a
  built artifact, all assessed against the same underlying objective.
- **Environment differentiation** — adjusting the physical or social
  setting: quiet workspace, flexible grouping, or reduced sensory
  load, so the environment isn't itself a barrier to the objective.
- **Ongoing readiness data** — differentiation only works when it's
  based on current information about where each learner actually is,
  not a fixed assumption set once at the start of a unit.

## Differentiating without lowering the objective

The point of differentiation is to vary the path to a shared
objective, not to quietly lower the objective for some students while
keeping it for others — a modified path that ends up teaching less
content isn't differentiation, it's a different, lesser goal wearing
the same label. The check is whether every version of the
differentiated task, however different in complexity or format, is
still built to demonstrate the same underlying learning objective.
This is what keeps differentiation distinct from a watered-down
assignment: a student working with a simpler text is still expected to
demonstrate the same reading-comprehension objective as a student
working with a harder one, just through a text matched to where they
currently are. Differentiation also has to stay responsive — readiness
groupings made in week one and never revisited stop reflecting where
students actually are by week six.

## Common pitfalls

- **Differentiation by activity only, not by objective** — giving
  different groups different worksheets without checking that each
  one still targets the same underlying learning goal produces
  activities that look differentiated but teach different things.
- **Static grouping** — assigning readiness groups once and never
  updating them as students grow, which turns a flexible tool into a
  fixed track.
- **Confusing differentiation with a formal accommodation plan** —
  treating what should be a documented, monitored
  [[individualized-education-program]] as an informal in-class
  adjustment risks missing a legal obligation.
- **Over-scaffolding** — providing so much support that a student
  never has to do the cognitive work the objective actually requires,
  which quietly lowers the bar rather than adjusting the path to it.
- **One-size differentiation** — applying the same three tiers to
  every lesson regardless of what the specific content and objective
  actually call for.
