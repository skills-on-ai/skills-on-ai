# Polish Language

Polski (Polish) is a West Slavic language, official in Poland and
spoken by roughly 40 million people. It's written in the Latin alphabet
extended with diacritical letters — ą, ć, ę, ł, ń, ó, ś, ź, ż — each a
distinct letter with its own sound and meaning, plus digraphs like cz,
sz, rz, and dz representing single sounds. Consonant clusters that look
unusual to English speakers (szcz, source of the classic tongue-twister
"w Szczebrzeszynie") are a normal, pronounceable part of the spelling
system, not typos.

## Seven grammatical cases

Polish nouns, adjectives, and pronouns change form depending on their
grammatical role in the sentence, through a system of seven cases:
nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental, locative, and
vocative. This is one of the more complex case systems among major
European languages — the same noun can take several different endings
depending on whether it's a subject, direct object, indirect object, or
object of a particular preposition, and adjectives modifying it must
match the noun's case, gender, and number. Getting case endings wrong is
one of the most common and most noticeable errors in non-native Polish
text.

## Gender and the masculine-personal split

Polish has three genders in the singular (masculine, feminine, neuter),
but the plural adds a further distinction that has no direct equivalent
in most other European languages: masculine-personal (referring to a
group containing at least one male person) versus non-masculine-personal
(everything else — mixed-gender groups without men, all-female groups,
animals, objects). This split drives different verb past-tense forms and
different adjective/pronoun endings in the plural: "widzieli" (they
saw, masculine-personal) versus "widziały" (they saw, non-masculine-
personal) for an otherwise identical sentence, depending purely on who
"they" refers to.

## Diacritics and text processing

The nine diacritical letters (ą, ć, ę, ł, ń, ó, ś, ź, ż) are distinct
letters, not accented variants that can be safely stripped: dropping or
substituting one changes or destroys the word's meaning (być "to be" vs.
byc, which isn't a word). ł in particular is pronounced like an English
"w," not like an English "l," and is easy for automated tools or
non-native writers to mishandle when normalizing text or transliterating
sloppily.

## Formal/informal address: Pan/Pani

Rather than a dedicated formal second-person pronoun, Polish uses a
structurally different approach: formal address uses the third person
combined with a title — Pan (Mr./sir) for a man, Pani (Mrs./Ms./madam)
for a woman — so "would you like..." formally becomes something like
"czy Pan/Pani chciałby/chciałaby..." (literally closer to "would the
gentleman/lady like..."), with the verb conjugated in the third person
and matching the addressee's gender. Informal address uses the ordinary
second-person "ty," used with family, friends, children, and increasingly
peers. This third-person-plus-title pattern is a genuinely different
mechanism from languages that simply swap in a separate formal pronoun
(like French vous or German Sie), and needs to be handled as such rather
than mapped onto a simple pronoun substitution.

## Common pitfalls

- **Getting noun/adjective case endings wrong** — since case marks
  grammatical role, an incorrect ending can make a sentence
  ungrammatical or genuinely ambiguous, not just awkward.
- **Ignoring the masculine-personal vs. non-masculine-personal split in
  plural verb and adjective forms** — using one form for a mixed group
  or an all-female group when the other is grammatically required.
- **Stripping or substituting diacritics** (treating ł as l, ż as z) —
  this changes word meaning and reads as illiterate or careless in
  formal Polish text, not merely accent-free.
- **Directly translating a formal pronoun from another language into a
  Polish second-person form** instead of switching to the Pan/Pani plus
  third-person-verb construction.
- **Using ty in contexts (business correspondence, addressing strangers
  or elders) where Pan/Pani formality is expected.**
