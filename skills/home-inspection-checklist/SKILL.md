# Home Inspection Checklist

A home inspection checklist is a systematic way to check a home's
physical condition, most often before a purchase, so that problems get
found and priced in before money changes hands rather than discovered
afterward. The same checklist discipline applies, in a lighter form,
before signing a [[lease-agreement]] or before a [[moving-checklist]]
move-in, wherever documenting a property's condition up front protects
whoever's about to be responsible for it.

## Key components

- **Structural and major systems, checked first** — roof, foundation,
  electrical, plumbing, and HVAC, since these are the most expensive
  categories to fix and the ones most likely to affect the decision to
  proceed or the price being paid.
- **Cosmetic issues, checked separately and weighted much lower** —
  paint, carpet, minor surface wear, tracked as a distinct list so they
  don't compete for attention with the categories that actually
  matter financially.
- **Documentation for anything found** — photos and written notes for
  every issue, taken at the time of inspection, since memory alone
  won't hold up later in a negotiation or a dispute.
- **Age and remaining life of major systems** — how old the roof, water
  heater, and HVAC unit are, and roughly how much service life is left,
  not just whether they currently work.
- **Exterior and site conditions** — grading and drainage away from the
  foundation, visible signs of water intrusion, and the condition of
  any structures beyond the main house (garage, deck, fence).

## Why structural issues need different weight than cosmetic ones

A foundation crack, an aging roof near the end of its life, or knob-
and-tube wiring can cost tens of thousands of dollars to address and
can affect whether the property is safe or insurable at all; a dated
paint color or worn carpet costs a weekend and a modest budget to fix.
A buyer who spends the inspection fixated on finishes while missing or
downplaying a structural issue has the priorities backwards relative
to what actually matters financially and safety-wise. Checking
structural and major-system items first, and treating them as a
separate, higher-weight list from cosmetic notes, keeps the inspection
focused on what could change the deal rather than what merely affects
first impressions.

## Common pitfalls

- **Cosmetic issues given the same attention as structural ones** — so
  a genuinely serious problem gets missed or under-weighted while
  attention goes to easily-fixed surface issues.
- **No photos or written documentation taken** — findings that exist
  only as memory can't be used to support a repair request, a price
  renegotiation, or a later dispute.
- **Inspection rushed to fit a tight closing timeline** — skipping or
  speeding through parts of the checklist to meet a deadline, instead
  of budgeting enough time to actually check everything on it, or
  pushing the timeline back when that's not possible.
- **Major systems' age never checked** — a system that currently works
  can still be near the end of its service life; noting only whether
  something functions today misses how soon it will need replacing.
- **No follow-up on items flagged for a specialist** — a general
  inspection that flags a possible issue (e.g. a suspect electrical
  panel) but never gets referred to a licensed specialist for a
  definitive answer before the decision is made.
