# Fundraising Event Planning

Fundraising event planning is the practice of designing an event whose
purpose is raising a specific amount of money, not merely bringing
people together. Every logistical decision — venue, program, ticket
price, ask structure — is made in service of a revenue target, which
is what separates it from general event planning: a fundraising event
that runs smoothly but misses its financial goal has still failed at
its actual job.

## Key components

- **Revenue target and cost budget** — a specific fundraising goal set
  before planning starts, against a full cost budget (venue, catering,
  materials), so the event's net (not gross) revenue is what actually
  gets tracked.
- **Revenue mix** — the combination of ticket sales, sponsorships,
  live or silent auction items, and a direct appeal during the event
  itself, planned as distinct revenue lines rather than one undifferentiated
  pot.
- **The ask moment** — a specific, planned point in the program (often
  after an emotional program element, before dessert or entertainment)
  where attendees are directly asked to give, rather than assuming
  ticket price alone covers the target.
- **Sponsor and donor recognition** — a tiered recognition plan
  (naming, signage, program mentions) that gives sponsors a reason to
  return next year, decided before sponsors are solicited, not
  improvised at the event.
- **Volunteer and staffing plan** — the people needed to run
  registration, the auction, and the program itself; see
  [[volunteer-management]] for recruiting and coordinating them.

## Net revenue is the only number that matters

A fundraising event can sell out, generate glowing feedback, and still
lose money once venue, catering, entertainment, and materials costs
are subtracted from what came in — a failure mode invisible if the
only number being tracked is gross ticket sales or total pledged at
the event. Every planning decision should be evaluated against net
revenue from the start: an elaborate venue or entertainment budget has
to be justified by the additional revenue it's expected to bring in,
not treated as a fixed cost of doing the event at all. This is also
why the ask moment matters as much as any logistical decision — a
well-run event with no planned direct appeal often raises meaningfully
less than a modest one with a clear, well-timed ask, because ticket
price alone rarely covers both costs and the actual fundraising goal.

## Common pitfalls

- **Tracking gross revenue, not net** — celebrating total dollars
  raised without subtracting the event's full cost hides whether the
  event actually made financial sense to run.
- **No planned ask moment** — assuming ticket sales alone will hit the
  target, with no deliberate point in the program where attendees are
  asked to give further, routinely underperforms events that build one
  in.
- **Underpriced sponsorship tiers** — offering high-visibility
  recognition without pricing it to reflect what it's actually worth
  to a sponsor leaves revenue on the table before the event even
  starts.
- **Volunteer roles improvised on the day** — registration, auction
  checkout, and program logistics without assigned, briefed volunteers
  creates bottlenecks exactly when attendees are forming their
  impression of the organization.
- **No post-event follow-up plan** — treating the event as the end of
  the relationship, rather than the start of one, wastes the goodwill
  and contact information the event generated; see [[donor-management]].
