# Core Fitness

Core training builds the strength and control of the trunk muscles —
abdominals, obliques, lower back, and deep stabilizers — that support
posture, transfer force between the upper and lower body, and protect
the spine during nearly every other physical activity.

## Beyond visible abs

Core training is often conflated with training visible abdominal
muscles for appearance, but its functional role is broader: a strong,
well-coordinated core stabilizes the spine during lifting, running,
and daily movement. Visible muscle definition depends heavily on
overall body fat levels, not just how much core-specific training is
done — a common source of misplaced expectations.

## Anti-extension, anti-rotation, and anti-lateral-flexion

Much of the core's real job is resisting unwanted movement, not
producing it — planks (anti-extension), Pallof presses
(anti-rotation), and side planks (anti-lateral-flexion) train this
stabilizing function directly, and are often more transferable to
real-world strength and injury prevention than repetitive spinal
flexion exercises like traditional sit-ups.

## How core strength supports other goals

A stronger, more stable core improves performance and reduces injury
risk in nearly every other type of training — heavier lifts,
better running economy, and more resilient movement generally — which
is why core work is commonly included as a supporting element within
broader programs rather than trained in isolation.

## Common pitfalls

- **Focusing purely on spinal-flexion exercises (crunches)** — these
  train a narrower function than anti-extension/rotation work and, for
  some people, can aggravate the lower back if overdone.
- **Expecting visible abs from core exercises alone** — visible
  definition depends significantly on overall body fat percentage;
  core exercises build the muscle but don't reveal it if it's covered
  by excess body fat.
- **Neglecting the core within general strength training** — assuming
  core work is only needed as a separate, isolated block, rather than
  recognizing that many compound lifts already demand significant core
  stability.
- **Poor bracing technique** — not learning to properly engage the
  core during heavier lifts limits both performance and spinal
  protection.
