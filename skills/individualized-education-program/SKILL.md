# Individualized Education Program

An individualized education program (IEP) is a formal, legally binding
document written for one student with a disability, specifying
measurable goals, the special-education and related services they'll
receive, and the accommodations or modifications that make general
education access possible. It sits above informal
[[differentiated-instruction]], which any teacher applies to any
learner without a legal process, and it's broader than a
[[student-behavior-intervention-plan]], which addresses only a
behavior pattern rather than the student's full educational needs.

## Key components

- **Present levels of performance** — a specific, evidence-based
  description of where the student currently stands academically and
  functionally, the baseline every goal is measured against.
- **Measurable annual goals** — specific, checkable targets tied
  directly to the student's identified needs, written so progress can
  actually be assessed rather than asserted.
- **Special education and related services** — the specific services
  (speech therapy, resource room instruction, counseling) the student
  will receive, with frequency and duration stated explicitly.
- **Accommodations and modifications** — the supports that make
  general-curriculum access possible (extended time, reduced-distraction
  setting, modified assignment length), distinguished clearly from
  each other since accommodations change how a student accesses
  content while modifications change what's actually expected.
- **Least restrictive environment placement** — a specific, justified
  decision about how much time the student spends in general education
  versus specialized settings, defaulting toward inclusion unless the
  student's needs require otherwise.
- **Progress monitoring plan** — how and how often progress toward
  each goal gets measured and reported back to the family.

## Why the legal process shapes the document

An IEP isn't just good individualized planning written down — it's a
legal document produced through a specific, procedurally required
process (evaluation, eligibility determination, a team meeting that
includes the family, and periodic review), and the document has to be
defensible against that process, not just pedagogically sound. That
means every goal needs to trace back to an identified need documented
in the evaluation, every service needs a stated rationale, and the
family's input has to be genuinely incorporated rather than presented
as a finished plan for signature. This is also why an IEP can't be
substituted with an informal differentiation plan, however well
designed — the legal protections and enforceability that come with
IEP status (and the review, communicated through moments like a
[[parent-teacher-conference]] or a dedicated IEP meeting) are the
entire point, not an optional formality layered on top of good
teaching.

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague, unmeasurable goals** — a goal like "will improve reading"
  gives no way to determine, at the review point, whether it was
  actually met.
- **Services listed without rationale** — including a service because
  it's routinely offered rather than because the student's documented
  needs call for it weakens the plan and its defensibility.
- **Family input treated as a formality** — presenting a completed
  plan for signature rather than genuinely incorporating the family's
  observations and priorities undermines both the legal process and
  the plan's actual fit to the student.
- **Accommodations confused with modifications** — blurring the line
  between changing how a student accesses content and changing what's
  expected of them can unintentionally lower the bar without that
  being a deliberate, documented decision.
- **No real progress monitoring** — writing measurable goals but never
  actually tracking and reporting progress against them defeats the
  purpose of having made them measurable.
