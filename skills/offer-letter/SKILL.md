# Offer Letter

An offer letter is the formal, written document an organization sends
a candidate after deciding to hire them: the specific title,
compensation, and start terms being offered to that person, and what
the offer is conditioned on. It's a binding-adjacent document — it
usually doesn't replace a full employment contract where one applies,
but candidates, courts, and the organization itself all treat its
stated terms as what was actually promised, so it needs to be precise.

## Key components

- **Title and level** — the specific title and level being offered,
  matching what was actually discussed and what the [[job-description]]
  defines for that level.
- **Compensation** — base pay, pay frequency, and any bonus, equity, or
  signing bonus, stated as specifically as the organization's practice
  allows rather than left to be clarified later.
- **Start date** — the agreed first day of employment.
- **Reporting line** — who the person reports to.
- **Contingencies** — conditions the offer depends on: background
  check, reference check, right-to-work or visa verification, drug
  screening where applicable, each named explicitly rather than assumed.
- **Employment status language** — at-will status or equivalent
  jurisdiction-specific language, stated in terms that match local
  employment law rather than copied from a template written for a
  different jurisdiction.
- **Offer expiration date** — a deadline by which the candidate must
  accept, so the offer doesn't stay open indefinitely.

## Offer letter vs. job posting and job description

The [[job-posting]] attracted the candidate and the [[job-description]]
defines what the role does; the offer letter is what comes after both,
addressed to one named person with terms specific to them — the actual
pay, start date, and conditions negotiated for this hire, not the
general role definition or the recruiting pitch that got them to apply.

## Common pitfalls

- **Verbal offer details not matching the written letter** — a
  recruiter's spoken number, start date, or title that differs from
  what actually lands in writing erodes trust immediately and can
  create a dispute about what was actually promised.
- **Missing contingencies** — extending an offer with no documented
  conditions, then having a background check or reference surface a
  problem after the person has already started, with no clean way to
  act on it.
- **No offer expiration date** — an open-ended offer lets a candidate
  hold it indefinitely while shopping other options, and leaves the
  organization unable to move on to a backup candidate if the first
  choice goes quiet.
- **Offer contradicts the job description** — a title or set of
  responsibilities in the letter that doesn't match the internal role
  definition it was supposedly hired against, creating confusion from
  day one.
- **Ambiguous employment-status language** — vague or copied-over
  wording can be read as creating an implied contract the organization
  didn't intend.
