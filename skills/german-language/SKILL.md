# German Language

German (Deutsch) is a Germanic language, official in Germany, Austria,
and Switzerland, and widely spoken as a minority or co-official language
elsewhere in Europe. Standard German (Hochdeutsch) is largely shared
across these countries for formal writing, but real regional differences
exist, especially in Switzerland.

## Case system

German marks a noun's grammatical role with one of four cases —
nominative (subject), accusative (direct object), dative (indirect
object), and genitive (possession) — which changes the form of the
article and often the adjective ending, and sometimes the noun itself.
This has no direct English equivalent (English case survives only in
pronouns: he/him/his). For example, "the dog" is "der Hund"
(nominative), "den Hund" (accusative), "dem Hund" (dative), and "des
Hundes" (genitive) — the article and even the noun's ending change
depending on its role in the sentence. Case errors are one of the most
common and noticeable mistakes in non-native or machine-generated
German.

## Grammatical gender

Every German noun has a grammatical gender — masculine (der), feminine
(die), or neuter (das) — that must be memorized per noun rather than
predicted from meaning. Some patterns help (many nouns ending in "-heit"
or "-keit" are feminine; diminutives ending in "-chen" or "-lein" are
always neuter regardless of the underlying noun's gender), but there is
no fully reliable general rule, and gender is not optional: it
determines article form and interacts with the case system, so getting
it wrong compounds into further errors down the sentence.

## Compound nouns

German freely forms long compound nouns by joining words together with
no space, which can produce very long single words:
"Rindfleischetikettierungsüberwachungsaufgabenübertragungsgesetz" (a
historical, if extreme, real example) or the more everyday
"Lebensversicherungsgesellschaft" (life insurance company). When
translating into German, prefer building a genuine compound over an
awkward multi-word phrase; when translating out of German, be prepared
to decompose a long compound into its constituent parts to render it in
English.

## Formal/informal address

German distinguishes formal "Sie" from informal "du" for second-person
address, and this affects verb conjugation, not just word choice — "Sie
sind" (formal, also identical in form to third-person plural) versus "du
bist" (informal) use different verb forms entirely. Choosing the wrong
register is a genuine social error, not a stylistic nuance: use "Sie" by
default for strangers, professional contexts, and anyone not explicitly
invited to switch to "du" ("das Du anbieten"), and "du" for family,
friends, children, and increasingly some informal workplace or online
contexts.

## Regional variation

Standard German is official in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, but
each has real differences in vocabulary, and Swiss Standard German has
a distinctive orthographic feature: it does not use the letter "ß"
(scharfes S / eszett) at all, writing "ss" instead even where German and
Austrian standard spelling would use "ß" (e.g. "Strasse" in
Switzerland vs. "Straße" in Germany/Austria). Austrian German also has
its own vocabulary for many everyday and administrative terms distinct
from both German and Swiss usage.

## Common pitfalls

- Guessing a noun's gender from its meaning in English or from a
  cognate's gender in another language, rather than using the noun's
  actual German gender.
- Getting case endings wrong on articles and adjectives, especially
  after prepositions that take a specific case (or one of two cases
  depending on motion vs. location).
- Using "ß" in Swiss German text, or omitting it in German/Austrian text
  where standard orthography requires it.
- Defaulting to "du" in a professional or unfamiliar context where "Sie"
  is expected, or conjugating verbs for the wrong register after
  choosing the correct pronoun.
- Translating a natural German compound noun as an awkward string of
  separate English-style words, or vice versa, forcing an unnatural
  compound into English.
