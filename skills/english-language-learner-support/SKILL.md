# English Language Learner Support

English language learner support plans how a student who has not yet
reached full proficiency in the language of instruction will access
grade-level academic content in that language. It sits alongside the
content objective, not in place of it: the goal is to keep grade-level
content within reach while the student is still acquiring the
language it's taught in, not to simplify the content itself down to
the student's current language level.

## Key components

- **Content objective** — what every student in the room, including
  this one, should learn from the lesson, stated the same way it would
  be for any other student.
- **Language objective** — the specific academic language skill (a
  sentence structure, a set of vocabulary, a discourse pattern) the
  student will practice while accessing the content, matched to their
  current proficiency level rather than assumed.
- **Comprehensible input strategies** — visuals, gestures, modeling,
  and simplified (not simplistic) sentence structure that make
  grade-level content understandable without watering down what's
  being taught.
- **Structured language scaffolds** — sentence frames, word banks, and
  graphic organizers that give the student a way to produce academic
  language they couldn't yet generate unprompted.
- **Native-language support** — bilingual glossaries, translated key
  terms, or peer/home-language pairing, used to build a bridge to the
  English content rather than to replace it.
- **Proficiency-level calibration** — matching the scaffold's
  intensity to where the student actually is (entering, developing,
  expanding, bridging), since a scaffold built for beginners
  under-challenges a student closer to fluency.

## Separating the language barrier from a content gap

The central diagnostic problem in supporting an English language
learner is telling apart a student who doesn't yet have the language
to show what they know from a student who genuinely hasn't learned the
content. These look identical on an assessment that requires written
English to demonstrate understanding — both produce a wrong or blank
answer — but they call for opposite responses. Treating a language gap
as a content gap sends a student back through material they've already
mastered, at a rate that neither engages them nor closes the actual
gap. Treating a content gap as a language gap, conversely, means
handing a student more scaffolding for content they haven't actually
learned, which doesn't help them access material that isn't there to
access.

The fix is to separate the check whenever possible: let the student
demonstrate content understanding through a channel that doesn't
depend on English production — a diagram, a demonstration, a
home-language explanation, a multiple-choice check — before concluding
that a struggle with the English-language version of the task reflects
a content problem rather than a language one. A language objective
that's explicit and separate from the content objective makes this
distinction visible in planning, not just in hindsight during
assessment.

## Common pitfalls

- **Content simplified instead of scaffolded** — cutting grade-level
  content down to match current language proficiency, rather than
  keeping the content and adding scaffolds to reach it, quietly lowers
  the ceiling on what the student is expected to learn.
- **No separate language objective** — without one, language support
  becomes an improvised, inconsistent response to whatever confusion
  shows up in the moment, instead of a planned part of the lesson.
- **One scaffold level for every student** — treating "English language
  learner" as a single category rather than a range of proficiency
  levels either over-scaffolds a student close to fluency or
  under-scaffolds one just starting out.
- **Silence read as comprehension** — a student who isn't yet
  comfortable producing spoken English may stay quiet regardless of
  whether they understood, so participation alone is an unreliable
  check.
- **Support that never fades** — scaffolds provided indefinitely
  without a plan to reduce them as proficiency grows keep a student
  dependent on support they may no longer need.
