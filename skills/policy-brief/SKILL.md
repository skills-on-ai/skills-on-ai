# Policy Brief

A policy brief is a short document that frames a policy issue, lays
out the realistic options, and recommends one of them to a specific
decision-maker. Its job is to be read fast and acted on, not to
demonstrate the full depth of research behind it — that belongs in a
separate report the brief can point to, not repeat.

## Key components

- **The issue, framed in one paragraph** — what the problem is, why it
  matters now, and who it affects, stated plainly enough that a reader
  with no prior context understands the stakes immediately.
- **The specific options considered** — a short, realistic set (often
  two to four), not every option in the abstract; each stated with its
  actual tradeoffs, not just its appeal.
- **A clear recommendation with its rationale** — which option is being
  proposed and why it beats the alternatives on the criteria that
  matter to this decision-maker (cost, political feasibility,
  timeline, risk).
- **An executive summary that alone conveys the recommendation** — a
  reader who reads nothing else should still walk away knowing what's
  being proposed and why; this is usually the first thing written and
  the last thing trimmed.
- **Supporting evidence, kept brief** — the key facts and figures that
  justify the recommendation, with fuller evidence and citations left
  to an appendix or a linked report rather than crowding the brief
  itself.

## Why writing for a specific, named decision-maker changes the brief

The same underlying issue reads very differently depending on who has
to act on it. A minister needs the political and public framing, the
headline cost, and a decision they can defend in five minutes; a
technical committee needs the methodology, the assumptions behind the
numbers, and enough detail to interrogate the recommendation. A brief
written for a generic "policymaker" audience tends to satisfy neither
reader well — it's too shallow for the technical reviewer and too
dense for the time-pressed decision-maker. Naming the actual audience
before writing determines what to include, what to cut, and what tone
to use throughout.

## Common pitfalls

- **Buried recommendation** — the reader has to reach the last page to
  find out what's actually being proposed, when the whole point of a
  brief is that the recommendation is findable in the first paragraph.
- **Written for a generic audience instead of the specific
  decision-maker who will actually read it** — a brief that tries to
  serve everyone ends up serving no one especially well.
- **Options presented with no real tradeoff analysis** — listing
  alternatives without weighing their costs, risks, or feasibility
  makes the final recommendation read as arbitrary rather than
  reasoned.
- **Evidence dump instead of a brief** — restating the full research
  report inside the brief defeats its purpose; a decision-maker who
  wanted the full evidence base would have read the report.
- **No stated timeline or next step** — a recommendation with no clear
  "do this, by when" leaves the decision-maker unsure what action is
  actually being asked of them.
