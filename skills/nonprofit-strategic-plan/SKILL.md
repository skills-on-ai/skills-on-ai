# Nonprofit Strategic Plan

A nonprofit strategic plan sets an organization's direction over
several years, organized around advancing its mission rather than
growing revenue or market share. Every goal in the plan has to trace
back to the mission it serves; a goal that would look reasonable on a
for-profit strategic plan — expand into a new market, grow revenue
20% — only belongs here if it's justified by the additional mission
impact it would produce, not pursued for its own sake.

## Key components

- **Theory of change** — an explicit statement of how the
  organization's activities are believed to lead to its intended
  impact, stated specifically enough that a goal can be checked
  against whether it actually advances that chain, not just whether
  it sounds related to the mission.
- **Multi-year goals tied to impact** — a small number of specific
  goals for the plan's time horizon, each stated in terms of the
  change it's meant to produce, not merely the activity it involves.
- **Resource and capacity assessment** — an honest accounting of what
  staffing, funding, and organizational capacity the plan's goals
  actually require, checked against what the organization currently
  has or can realistically build.
- **Stakeholder input** — input gathered from staff, board, and
  ideally the community or population the organization serves, since a
  plan built without them tends to reflect what leadership assumes
  matters rather than what actually does.
- **Board approval and accountability** — formal adoption by the
  board, whose ongoing job under [[nonprofit-board-governance]] includes
  holding the executive accountable to the plan's goals, not just
  approving the document once.

## Mission fit is the actual constraint

A for-profit strategic plan can pursue almost any opportunity that
grows revenue or market position; a nonprofit strategic plan can't —
every opportunity, however financially attractive, has to be checked
against whether it actually advances the mission, and a nonprofit that
chases revenue-attractive opportunities disconnected from its theory
of change risks mission drift even while its balance sheet improves.
This is why a nonprofit strategic plan needs an explicit theory of
change stated up front: without one, there's no test available for
whether a proposed goal belongs in the plan at all, and the planning
process defaults to whatever initiatives are easiest to fund or
easiest to justify after the fact, rather than what the organization's
own logic says will produce the most impact.

## Common pitfalls

- **Goals stated as activities, not outcomes** — "expand our program
  to two more cities" describes an activity, not the impact it's
  meant to produce, and gives no way to check afterward whether it
  actually worked.
- **No theory of change to check goals against** — without an explicit
  statement of how the organization believes its work produces impact,
  any goal that sounds mission-adjacent can get added to the plan with
  no real test of whether it belongs.
- **Plan built without stakeholder input** — a plan drafted solely by
  leadership, without input from staff, board, or the community served,
  tends to reflect assumptions rather than what's actually needed.
- **Resource needs assessed after goals are set** — committing to
  ambitious goals before honestly assessing whether the organization
  has, or can realistically build, the capacity to pursue them sets the
  plan up to stall mid-implementation.
- **Plan adopted and then shelved** — a strategic plan the board
  approves once and never revisits provides no actual accountability
  for whether the organization is on track.
