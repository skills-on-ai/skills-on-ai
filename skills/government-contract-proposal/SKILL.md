# Government Contract Proposal

A government contract proposal is a bid submitted in response to a
government solicitation for goods or services. It shares the basic
shape of a commercial [[sales-proposal]] — a solution, a scope, a
price — but operates under a much more formal set of rules: the
solicitation itself states exactly what a compliant bid must contain
and how it will be scored, and evaluators are often required to follow
that process to the letter rather than exercise judgment about a
bid's overall merit. The specific procurement rules, evaluation
process, and any set-aside programs for particular categories of
bidder (small business, veteran-owned, local supplier) vary
significantly by country and level of government — a US federal
procurement under the FAR, a state or municipal RFP, and a
solicitation under another country's procurement law all impose
different formal requirements.

## Key components

- **Strict compliance with every stated requirement in the
  solicitation** — every mandatory form, certification, page limit,
  and submission deadline met exactly as specified, since government
  evaluators are often required to disqualify a technically
  non-compliant bid regardless of how strong it otherwise is.
- **A response structured around the solicitation's own stated
  evaluation criteria** — addressed point by point, using the
  solicitation's own section headings and terminology, rather than a
  generic capabilities pitch that leaves the evaluator to hunt for
  what's actually being scored.
- **Pricing built to the exact required format** — the specific
  structure, units, and breakdown the solicitation calls for, since an
  inconsistent or reformatted pricing table is a common and often
  fatal disqualification reason.
- **Any required certifications or eligibility documentation** —
  set-aside program eligibility, insurance, licensing, or other
  qualifying paperwork the solicitation specifies must accompany the
  bid.

## Why strict compliance matters more here than in commercial sales

A commercial buyer can waive a minor omission in a sales proposal and
still choose the seller they like best. A government evaluator
usually can't: procurement processes are typically formal and
auditable specifically so that a losing bidder can be told, and can
verify, that the process was fair — and a losing competitor who
suspects an evaluator bent the rules to favor a non-compliant bid can
often file a formal protest that stalls or reverses the award. That
structural risk is why evaluators are frequently required to
disqualify a non-compliant bid even when they personally think it was
the strongest one: following the rule protects the process, not just
this one decision.

## Common pitfalls

- **A generic capabilities pitch that doesn't map to the stated
  evaluation criteria** — a well-written proposal that never
  explicitly addresses how it meets each scored criterion gets marked
  down or excluded on a technicality, regardless of underlying quality.
- **A technical compliance requirement missed entirely** — a missing
  certification, an unsigned form, or a page limit exceeded results in
  automatic disqualification no matter how strong the price or the
  technical approach is.
- **Pricing submitted in a different format than required** — treating
  a required pricing structure as a suggestion rather than a
  requirement is often treated by the evaluator as disqualifying, not
  as a minor formatting issue.
- **Deadline treated loosely** — a bid submitted even slightly after
  the stated deadline is typically rejected outright, with no
  exception for being close.
- **Assuming one country's or one agency's procurement rules transfer
  to another** — a compliance checklist built for one solicitation
  process doesn't reliably cover the requirements of a different
  government's or agency's process.
