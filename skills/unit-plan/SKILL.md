# Unit Plan

A unit plan sits between a single lesson and a whole course: it groups
a sequence of lessons around a shared objective, sequences them so
each one builds toward it, and ends in an assessment that checks
whether the unit's objective — not just any one lesson's — was
actually reached. It's the layer where individual [[lesson-plan]]s get
coordinated into something bigger than any one of them, without yet
being the full scope of a [[curriculum-design]].

## Key components

- **Unit-level objective** — what learners should be able to do by the
  end of the whole unit, specific enough that any individual lesson in
  the unit can be checked against whether it's actually contributing
  to it.
- **Lesson sequence** — the individual lessons that make up the unit,
  ordered so each builds on what came before, with each lesson's own
  objective a sub-step toward the unit objective.
- **Prior knowledge check** — a way to confirm, before the unit starts,
  that learners actually have the prerequisite knowledge the sequence
  assumes.
- **Formative checkpoints** — checks distributed across the unit's
  lessons, not only at the end, so a gap in an early lesson surfaces
  while there's still time in the unit to address it.
- **Unit assessment** — a culminating check, at the end of the unit,
  of the unit-level objective specifically — not a sum of each
  lesson's individual content, but a check that the pieces actually
  came together.
- **Pacing across the unit's actual time** — a realistic mapping of the
  lesson sequence to the number of sessions actually available for the
  unit.

## Why the unit needs its own objective, not just a list of lessons

A collection of lessons on a related topic isn't automatically a unit
in any meaningful sense unless something ties them together beyond
subject matter — a unit-level objective that no single lesson fully
covers but that the sequence, taken together, builds toward. Without
that objective, a unit plan degrades into a syllabus: a list of topics
in order, each taught reasonably well on its own, with no assurance
that they add up to anything more than the sum of their parts.

The unit assessment is where this distinction actually gets tested.
An assessment built from a checklist of what each lesson covered
measures coverage, not integration — a student can pass it by
remembering each lesson separately without ever connecting them. An
assessment built from the unit's own objective requires the student to
draw on multiple lessons together, which is the actual signal that the
sequence functioned as a unit rather than as a string of loosely
related lessons.

## Common pitfalls

- **No unit-level objective, only a topic** — "the unit on fractions"
  names a subject, not an objective; without a specific unit-level
  objective there's no way to check whether the sequence of lessons
  actually builds toward anything beyond covering the topic.
- **Unit assessment is just a combined quiz** — testing each lesson's
  content separately in one longer test measures recall of individual
  lessons, not whether the unit's own objective was reached.
- **Lessons sequenced by topic order, not dependency** — ordering
  lessons the way a textbook chapter happens to be organized, rather
  than by what each lesson actually requires learners to already know.
- **No prior-knowledge check before the unit starts** — assuming
  prerequisites are in place without confirming it risks the whole
  sequence resting on a gap that surfaces too late to address cheaply.
- **Pacing set by the calendar, not the content** — fitting the
  sequence to a fixed number of weeks regardless of how the actual
  content divides, rather than adjusting the unit's scope to the time
  genuinely available.
