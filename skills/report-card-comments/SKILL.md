# Report Card Comments

Report card comments are short, individualized narrative summaries of
a student's progress, written under real constraints: dozens of
students, a character or line limit per comment, and no chance for the
parent to ask a follow-up question the way they could in a
conversation. The comment has to stand on its own, say something
specific to that student, and land in the time it takes to write one
of many.

## Key components

- **Specific evidence, not a template** — a detail unique to the
  student (a skill they've grown in, a habit that's holding them back)
  rather than a generic sentence that could describe any student in
  the class.
- **Balance of strength and growth area** — both what the student is
  doing well and what they need to work on, since a comment that's all
  praise or all concern gives the parent an incomplete and often
  misleading picture.
- **Actionable framing for the growth area** — phrased as something
  the student or parent can actually do something about, not just a
  label ("needs to improve organization" versus "benefits from
  writing down homework before leaving class").
- **Tone appropriate for the audience** — language a parent, not just
  another teacher, will understand and receive well, since jargon or a
  clinical tone can read as colder than intended.
- **Consistency with the grade** — a comment that doesn't contradict
  the numeric or letter grade sitting next to it, since a mismatch
  (glowing comment, low grade) reads as confusing or careless.

## Writing from evidence, at volume, without losing specificity

The real difficulty in report card comments isn't knowing what to say
about any one student — it's saying something true and specific about
each of thirty students in a row without the comments blurring into
interchangeable boilerplate by the tenth one. The practical fix is
keeping a running, dated log of specific observations through the
term — a comment, a moment, a piece of work — rather than trying to
reconstruct a term's worth of detail from memory the night before
comments are due. A comment written from a specific logged
observation takes barely longer to write than a generic one and reads
completely differently to the parent receiving it.

Reusable language for the structural parts of a comment (the opening,
the transition into the growth area, the closing) is fine and doesn't
undermine specificity, as long as the actual content in the middle —
the evidence and the framing of what's being said about it — is
particular to that student. The pitfall isn't reusing sentence
scaffolding; it's reusing whole comments with a name swapped in.

## Common pitfalls

- **Generic enough to fit any student** — a comment that could be
  copy-pasted onto a different student's report card without anyone
  noticing has failed the one job a comment has.
- **All strength or all concern** — an unbalanced comment gives a
  parent either false reassurance or an unqualified negative
  impression, neither of which matches a real student's progress.
- **Vague growth area** — "needs to try harder" gives a parent nothing
  to act on; a specific, concrete behavior does.
- **Written entirely from memory at the deadline** — without a running
  log, late-term comments tend to reflect only the most recent
  impression of the student, not the whole term.
- **Contradicts the grade next to it** — an enthusiastic comment paired
  with a low grade, or the reverse, undermines trust in both.
