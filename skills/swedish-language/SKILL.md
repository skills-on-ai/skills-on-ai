# Swedish Language

Swedish (svenska) is a North Germanic language, official in Sweden and
one of Finland's official languages. It is closely related to Norwegian
and Danish, with a good deal of mutual intelligibility among the three,
especially in writing.

## Grammatical gender

Swedish nouns have one of two grammatical genders — common (en-words)
and neuter (ett-words) — a simplified system compared to German's three
genders (der/die/das). Gender determines the indefinite article ("en
bil", a car, common; "ett hus", a house, neuter) and the adjective
ending ("en stor bil", a big car; "ett stort hus", a big house). As in
German, gender must generally be memorized per noun rather than
predicted from meaning, though the common gender covers the large
majority of nouns.

## Definite article as a suffix

Swedish attaches its definite article as a suffix directly onto the
noun, rather than using a separate word before it the way English uses
"the": "bil" (a car) becomes "bilen" (the car), and "hus" (a house)
becomes "huset" (the house). A separate preposed definite article
("den"/"det") is also used together with the suffix when the noun has a
preceding adjective ("den stora bilen", the big car), so both patterns
need to be handled correctly rather than treating the suffix alone as
the whole story.

## Pitch accent

Swedish has a pitch accent (tonal accent) system in which the same word
can carry one of two different pitch patterns that distinguish meaning
or grammatical form — for example "anden" can mean "the duck" or "the
spirit" depending on which pitch accent is used. This is a feature
largely absent from English, and while it matters most for spoken
Swedish, it's worth being aware of when transcribing speech or
explaining why two identically spelled words are pronounced differently.

## Formality/register

Modern Swedish has undergone a historical shift ("du-reformen," the
du-reform, from the mid-20th century onward) away from formal address:
it now mostly uses "du" (the informal second person) universally,
including with strangers, in customer service, and in many professional
contexts, with formal address ("ni," or third-person circumlocutions)
now uncommon and often perceived as old-fashioned or overly distant.
This is the reverse of the trend in many other European languages
(compare German's Sie/du distinction, still very much in active use):
defaulting to a formal register in Swedish can read as unusually stiff
or even mildly comic rather than appropriately polite, so "du" is the
safe default for most contemporary writing.

## Regional variation

Standard Swedish (rikssvenska) is largely shared across Sweden and
Finland-Swedish contexts for formal writing, though Finland-Swedish has
some distinct vocabulary and idiom influenced by Finnish, and spoken
Swedish varies noticeably by region (e.g. Scanian in the south, Norrland
dialects in the north) in ways that mostly don't affect standard written
text.

## Common pitfalls

- Guessing common vs. neuter gender from a noun's meaning rather than
  its actual lexical gender, producing a wrong article or adjective
  ending.
- Treating the definite article only as a suffix and forgetting the
  additional preposed "den"/"det" required when an adjective precedes
  the noun.
- Defaulting to an overly formal register ("ni" or elaborate
  circumlocutions) by analogy with other European languages' tu/vous-
  style distinctions, when contemporary Swedish expects "du."
- Ignoring pitch accent as irrelevant to English speakers, when it
  actually distinguishes meaning between otherwise identically spelled
  words in speech or transcription contexts.
- Assuming Swedish, Norwegian, and Danish are simply interchangeable
  because of their mutual intelligibility — vocabulary, spelling, and
  idiom still differ enough that text should be written in one language
  deliberately, not blended.
