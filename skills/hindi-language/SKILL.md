# Hindi Language

Hindi (हिन्दी) is an Indo-Aryan language written in the Devanagari
script. It is one of India's official languages at the union
(federal) level, alongside English, and India additionally recognizes
many other regional languages constitutionally — Hindi is widely
spoken and understood, especially across northern India, but it is not
the sole or universal language of the country.

## Writing system

Devanagari is written left-to-right and is an abugida: each consonant
letter carries an inherent default vowel (short "a") unless that
vowel is changed or removed by an attached vowel sign (mātrā) or a
special mark (virāma/halant) that suppresses the inherent vowel
entirely. This differs from an alphabet like Latin script, where
vowels and consonants are separate letters of equal status, and from
an abjad like Arabic, where vowels are mostly left unwritten —
Devanagari writes vowels but attaches most of them to consonants as
modifying signs rather than standalone letters. Consonant clusters are
often written as conjunct forms (ligatures combining two or more
consonant shapes), which can look visually distinct from either
consonant alone and matters for font and rendering support.

## Grammatical gender

Hindi nouns are grammatically masculine or feminine (no neuter), and
this gender governs agreement across verbs, adjectives, and
postpositions associated with the noun. Unlike Arabic or Russian,
Hindi gender frequently isn't predictable from a word's meaning alone,
though many masculine nouns end in -आ (ā) and many feminine nouns end
in -ई (ī) as a rough pattern with real exceptions. Verb forms in
particular change based on the gender (and number) of the subject or
object depending on sentence construction, which has no parallel in
English verb conjugation.

## Postpositions

Hindi uses postpositions rather than prepositions: grammatical markers
that indicate relationships like "in," "on," "to," or "of" come after
the noun they relate to, not before it. This also interacts with case
marking on the noun itself — nouns often take an oblique form before a
postposition. A word-for-word English-to-Hindi translation that simply
moves an English preposition in front of the noun will be
grammatically wrong; the marker has to move to the end of the noun
phrase and the noun's form may need to change too.

## Formality and the second person

Hindi has three second-person pronoun levels rather than a single
formal/informal split: तू (tū) is intimate/informal, used only with
very close relations or, in some contexts, as deliberately
disrespectful; तुम (tum) is informal but respectful, used with peers,
younger family, and friends; आप (āp) is the formal/respectful form,
used with elders, strangers, and in professional contexts. Each level
takes its own distinct verb conjugation, not just a different pronoun
word, so picking the right level affects the whole sentence. When in
doubt for an unfamiliar or professional context, आप is the safe
default.

## Hindi vs. Urdu

Hindi and Urdu are closely related and largely mutually intelligible
in casual, everyday speech — the core grammar and much everyday
vocabulary overlap heavily. They diverge sharply, though: Urdu is
written in the Perso-Arabic script (right-to-left) rather than
Devanagari, and at more formal or literary registers Urdu draws more
heavily on Persian and Arabic vocabulary while formal Hindi draws more
heavily on Sanskrit — the two can become quite distinct at that level.
Worth flagging explicitly to whoever requested the task if the actual
audience is Urdu-speaking (e.g. Pakistan, or Urdu-speaking communities
elsewhere) — the script and much formal vocabulary genuinely differ,
even though spoken casual Hindi and Urdu are close.

## Common pitfalls

- Translating English prepositions in place rather than converting
  them to postpositions after the noun (and adjusting the noun to its
  oblique form where required).
- Getting verb or adjective gender agreement wrong, especially when the
  subject's gender isn't obvious from the noun's ending.
- Defaulting to तुम or तू in a formal/professional context where आप is
  expected, or using the wrong verb conjugation for the pronoun level
  chosen.
- Producing text in Hindi when the actual audience or context calls
  for Urdu (or vice versa) — check script and audience before assuming
  Hindi is the right target.
- Mis-forming Devanagari conjunct consonant clusters, which can render
  as broken or visually wrong glyphs in systems with weak font
  support.
