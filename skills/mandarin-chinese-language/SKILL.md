# Mandarin Chinese Language

Mandarin Chinese (普通话 Pǔtōnghuà in the mainland, 國語 Guóyǔ in Taiwan) is
the most widely spoken variety of Chinese and the official language of
mainland China, Taiwan, and Singapore, and one of the official languages
of Hong Kong. It is a tonal, analytic language written with Chinese
characters rather than an alphabet.

## Writing system: two scripts, not one

Chinese text uses one of two character sets, and they are not
interchangeable:

- **Simplified characters** (简体字) — used in mainland China and
  Singapore, and the default for most PRC government, media, and
  software text.
- **Traditional characters** (繁體字) — used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and
  Macau, and generally preferred by overseas Chinese communities with
  roots in those regions.

Many characters differ visibly between the two sets (国 vs. 國, 学 vs.
學), and some words differ in vocabulary as well as script, not just
character shape (e.g. "software" is 软件 in the mainland, 軟體 in
Taiwan). Always determine the intended audience/region before writing or
converting text — treating the two as freely interchangeable, or
mechanically converting one to the other character-by-character without
checking vocabulary, produces text that reads as wrong or foreign to
native readers of the other variant.

## Tone

Standard Mandarin has four main tones plus a neutral (unstressed) tone.
The same syllable spoken with a different pitch contour is a different
word with a different meaning — tone is not emphasis or intonation, it
is a core part of the word's identity, comparable to a consonant or
vowel in English. Tone matters most in **pinyin** (the standard Latin
romanization), where tone marks (mā, má, mǎ, mà) or tone numbers (ma1,
ma2, ma3, ma4) distinguish otherwise identical syllables and should
never be dropped in careful romanized text. Characters themselves do not
mark tone visually — a reader who knows the character already knows its
tone, so tone is a pinyin/pronunciation concern, not a character-writing
one.

## No conjugation, plurals, or gender — but measure words are required

Mandarin verbs do not conjugate for tense, person, or number, and nouns
have no grammatical gender and are not marked for plural the way English
nouns are (adding -s). Instead:

- Tense/aspect is carried by separate particles (了 le for completion,
  过 guo for past experience, 在 zài for ongoing action) or simply by
  context/time words, not verb endings.
- A number modifying a noun requires a **measure word (classifier)**
  between them — 一个人 (yí gè rén, "one person") not a bare number plus
  noun; different noun categories take different measure words (张
  zhāng for flat objects, 本 běn for bound volumes, 条 tiáo for long
  thin things), and picking the wrong one reads as a clear error.
- Word order (subject-verb-object, with topic-comment structures also
  common) and context, not inflection, carry most grammatical meaning.

## Formality and register

Mandarin has no separate honorific verb forms the way Japanese or Korean
does. Formality is instead carried through vocabulary choice, sentence
structure, and a small set of politeness markers: 您 (nín) as a
respectful second-person pronoun versus plain 你 (nǐ), 请 (qǐng, "please")
before requests, and more elaborate/indirect phrasing in formal or
written contexts versus blunter, particle-heavy phrasing (啊, 吧, 呢) in
casual speech. Written/formal register also favors four-character set
phrases (成语 chéngyǔ) and more literary vocabulary that would sound
stiff or pretentious in casual conversation.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing Simplified characters for a Taiwan/Hong Kong audience (or vice
  versa) without checking, or converting between the two script-only
  without also checking for regional vocabulary differences.
- Dropping tone marks in pinyin, which can leave a romanized word
  genuinely ambiguous between several unrelated meanings.
- Adding an English-style plural marker to a noun, or omitting a
  measure word between a number and a noun.
- Using 你 (nǐ) in a context that calls for the more respectful 您 (nín),
  such as addressing an elder, a customer, or a formal audience.
- Assuming Mandarin is representative of all "Chinese" — Cantonese and
  other varieties have different vocabulary, grammar particles, and
  (for Cantonese specifically) a distinct informal written form.
