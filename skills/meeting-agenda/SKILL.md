# Meeting Agenda

A meeting agenda is the document sent before a meeting that states its
purpose and lists the topics to cover, in order, with a time allocation
and an owner for each. Its job is to let attendees prepare and to give
the meeting itself something concrete to converge on, rather than
existing as a formality nobody reads.

This skill covers agenda-writing as a craft. For the specific meeting
formats that use an agenda, see [[one-on-one-meeting]],
[[all-hands-meeting]], [[weekly-planning-meeting]],
[[yearly-planning-meeting]], and [[skip-level-meeting]] — this skill is
what makes any of those agendas good, not a format of its own.

## Key components

- **Purpose or desired outcome** — a stated sentence on why the meeting
  is happening and what it needs to produce (a decision, a shared
  understanding, a plan) — not just a list of subjects to mention.
- **Timed items in priority order** — each topic gets an explicit time
  box, and the most important items go first, so a meeting that runs
  short or long still covers what matters most.
- **An owner per item** — the person presenting or driving discussion on
  that item, so attendees know who to direct questions to and who is
  accountable for it being ready.
- **Pre-reading, noted explicitly** — if attendees are expected to read
  something before arriving (a doc, a proposal, prior [[meeting-minutes]]),
  the agenda says so and links it, rather than assuming everyone already
  saw it.
- **Attendee list** — who is expected, and ideally why each person is
  needed, so someone who isn't required for a given item can tell.

## Why sending it in advance matters

An agenda only does its job if it arrives with real lead time before the
meeting, not minutes before it starts. Advance notice lets attendees
prepare their input instead of forming it live in the room, which
produces shallower discussion and slower decisions. It also lets someone
realize they don't actually need to attend a given item (or the whole
meeting) and free up that time — a courtesy that only works if they see
the agenda early enough to act on it.

## Scope discipline

An agenda is not the place to list everything that could conceivably be
discussed. Each item should earn its slot by tying back to the stated
purpose; a topic that doesn't need this specific group of people in this
specific meeting belongs in an email, an async doc, or a different
meeting. A shorter agenda that stays on time is more useful than a long
one that guarantees overrun.

## Common pitfalls

- **Sent minutes before the meeting** — defeats the entire purpose;
  attendees can't prepare, and the agenda functions as a formality
  rather than a working document.
- **No time allocations** — without a time box per item, one topic
  (usually whichever generates the most discussion) eats the rest of the
  meeting and later items get rushed or dropped.
- **Items with no stated purpose** — "discuss Q3 roadmap" gives the
  discussion nothing to converge toward; "decide which two Q3 roadmap
  bets to fund" does.
- **No owner per item** — leaves it unclear who was supposed to prepare
  material or drive the topic, and the item often turns out half-ready.
- **Agenda padded to fill the calendar slot** — including items that
  don't need the full group just to use up a scheduled hour wastes
  everyone's time and trains people to skim future agendas.
- **Agenda ignored once the meeting starts** — a document nobody
  actually follows in the room provides no benefit over having none.
