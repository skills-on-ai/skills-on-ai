# Medical Advance Directive

> ⚠️ **Ask your doctor or health care professional. This information is AI generated for education and NOT medical advice.**

An advance directive is a document in which a person states their
wishes for future medical treatment, or names a health care proxy
(also called a health care agent) to make treatment decisions on their
behalf, for use if they later become unable to communicate those
wishes themselves. It is distinct from a general
[[last-will-and-testament]] or a broad [[power-of-attorney]] — those
cover asset distribution and wider legal or financial authority, while
an advance directive is specifically about medical treatment decisions
— though the two are often prepared together as part of
[[estate-planning]].

## Key components

- **Specific treatment preferences** — stated concretely (for example,
  wishes regarding life-sustaining treatment, mechanical ventilation,
  artificial nutrition, or resuscitation) rather than only a vague
  general statement about what the person would or wouldn't want.
- **A named health care proxy/agent** — a specific person authorized
  to make treatment decisions not explicitly covered by the stated
  preferences, chosen because they understand the person's values and
  are willing and able to act on them under pressure.
- **Accessibility at the moment of need** — the document made available
  to the people who will actually need it in an emergency: family, and
  the clinicians who will be treating the patient. A directive
  correctly completed and then filed away where nobody can find it
  provides no guidance when it matters.
- **A date and, ideally, a review history** — since preferences can
  reasonably change after a major diagnosis, a significant health
  event, or simply the passage of years.

## Why accessibility matters as much as content

An advance directive is only useful at the moment someone else needs
to act on it — typically a medical emergency, when the patient cannot
speak for themselves and there is no time to search for a document.
An advance directive nobody can locate at that moment provides no more
practical guidance than not having one at all, no matter how carefully
it was written. Getting a copy to family, to the named proxy, and into
the patient's medical record (or wherever the treating institution
keeps such documents) is as much a part of doing this correctly as
writing the preferences themselves.

## Common pitfalls

- **Preferences stated too vaguely to guide a real decision** — a
  phrase like "nothing heroic" sounds clear in the abstract but gives
  a clinician or proxy little to act on when a specific, concrete
  decision has to be made at 2 a.m.
- **Completed but never shared** — the document exists, but the
  family, the named proxy, and the person's usual treating clinicians
  have never seen it and don't know it exists or what it says.
- **Never revisited after a major health change** — a directive written
  years earlier, before a new diagnosis or a significant change in
  health or values, that no longer reflects what the person would
  actually choose now.
- **No named proxy, or a named proxy who doesn't know they've been
  named** — leaving no one positioned to interpret the directive for
  situations it doesn't explicitly cover.
- **Confusing an advance directive with a [[medical-do-not-resuscitate-order]]** —
  a directive is a statement of the patient's own wishes; a DNR is a
  clinician-written medical order, often but not always informed by a
  directive.

## A note on jurisdiction and legal validity

What form an advance directive must take, what makes it legally valid,
who may serve as a proxy, and how clinicians must honor it all vary by
country and state or region. Some jurisdictions require witnesses or
notarization; others use specific statutory forms. This is general
educational guidance on the structure and purpose of advance
directives, not legal advice for a specific person's situation. Always
follow institutional policy and consult a qualified lawyer to confirm
what makes a directive valid and enforceable in the relevant
jurisdiction.
