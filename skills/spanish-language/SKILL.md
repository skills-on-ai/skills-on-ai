# Spanish Language

Español (Spanish) is a Romance language, official in Spain and in most
of Latin America, and one of the most-spoken languages in the world.
It's written in the Latin alphabet plus ñ, with acute accents (á, é,
í, ó, ú) marking stress or distinguishing otherwise-identical words
(tú "you" vs. tu "your"; él "he" vs. el "the").

## Grammatical gender

Every noun is masculine or feminine, and articles, adjectives, and many
pronouns must agree with it: el gato negro (the black cat, masculine),
la mesa negra (the black table, feminine). Gender is mostly but not
perfectly predictable from the ending (-o tends masculine, -a tends
feminine — el problema and la mano are common exceptions). Plural forms
also need agreement: los gatos negros, las mesas negras. Gender-neutral
language for people (e.g. using -e or @/x endings, or "elle" instead of
él/ella) is used in some communities but isn't standard in formal or
institutional writing.

## Formal/informal address: tú, usted, and vos

Spanish distinguishes informal and formal "you" with different pronouns
and verb conjugations. Tú is the informal singular used with peers,
family, and children in most of Spain and much of Latin America; usted
is the formal singular, used with strangers, elders, and in professional
contexts, and takes third-person verb forms. A third form, vos, replaces
tú as the everyday informal singular in Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay,
and parts of Central America, with its own distinct verb conjugation
(vos tenés rather than tú tienes) — this is a real three-way regional
split, not just a stylistic variant of tú.

## European vs. Latin American Spanish

Spain uses vosotros as the informal plural "you," conjugated with its
own verb forms and distinct from the formal plural ustedes. Latin
American Spanish has dropped vosotros entirely — ustedes covers both
formal and informal plural "you" everywhere in Latin America. Vocabulary
also diverges notably: coche vs. carro/auto (car), ordenador vs.
computadora (computer), móvil vs. celular (mobile phone), and many more
pairs. When translating, ask (or infer from context) which variant is
wanted — a translation into "Spanish" without a target region defaults
ambiguously and can read as noticeably foreign to either audience.

## Inverted punctuation

Standard Spanish orthography opens questions and exclamations with an
inverted mark — ¿ and ¡ — in addition to the closing ? and !: ¿Cómo
estás? ¡Qué bien! This isn't optional stylistic flourish; omitting the
opening mark is a real orthographic error in formal writing, even though
informal digital communication (texting, social media) increasingly
drops it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Dropping the opening ¿ or ¡** — common in casual digital text but
  incorrect in any formal or edited Spanish writing.
- **Mixing vosotros and ustedes conventions** within one text meant for
  a single regional audience, or using vosotros in text meant for a
  Latin American audience, where it will read as distinctly Spanish
  (from Spain) or archaic.
- **Ignoring vos conjugation** when writing for Argentina, Uruguay, or
  similar regions — using tú forms there is understood but sounds
  foreign or textbook-like rather than natural.
- **Getting noun gender wrong on exceptions** — nouns like el problema,
  el día, or la mano don't follow the usual -o/-a pattern.
- **Using European vocabulary in a Latin American context or vice
  versa** (coche/carro, ordenador/computadora) without checking which
  audience the text targets.
