# Restorative Classroom Circle

A restorative classroom circle is a structured process where a class
or a smaller group sits together, usually literally in a circle, and
takes turns speaking to a shared prompt — to repair harm after a
conflict, to process something that affected the group, or simply to
build the relationships a classroom runs on. It replaces a top-down
punitive response with a process where the people affected talk
through what happened and what needs to happen next, together.

## Key components

- **Clear purpose** — whether the circle is a proactive
  community-building circle or a responsive one addressing specific
  harm, decided before it starts, since the two need different framing
  and different participants.
- **A talking piece or turn structure** — a physical object or a clear
  turn-taking rule that ensures one person speaks at a time and
  everyone gets a turn, so the circle doesn't collapse into whoever
  speaks loudest.
- **An opening and closing ritual** — a consistent way the circle
  starts and ends, which signals that what's said inside it is
  different from ordinary classroom talk and deserves that
  seriousness.
- **A well-chosen prompt** — a question specific enough to focus the
  conversation but open enough that it doesn't presuppose the answer
  or corner any one participant.
- **Agreements, not just discussion** — for a harm-repair circle, a
  concrete agreement about what happens next that the people affected
  helped shape, not just an airing of what happened.
- **Facilitator neutrality** — the adult running the circle guides the
  process without directing the outcome, which is what separates this
  from a teacher delivering a verdict.

## Responding to harm without assigning a punishment

The core difference between a restorative circle and a traditional
disciplinary response is what question gets asked. A punitive response
asks who broke which rule and what consequence fits; a restorative
circle asks who was affected, how, and what needs to happen to make it
right — a question that keeps the people actually involved in the
conversation instead of routing the incident through an adult who
decides on their behalf. This doesn't mean there's no accountability;
it means the accountability is worked out with the people harmed,
often producing an outcome — an apology, a repair, a changed behavior —
that a fixed disciplinary code wouldn't have generated and that the
person harmed had a hand in shaping.

This approach depends on trust that's built before it's needed. A
class that only sees a circle when something has gone wrong
experiences it as a disguised disciplinary hearing, which undermines
the openness the format depends on. Community-building circles run
regularly, on ordinary days with no incident behind them, are what
make a harm-repair circle credible when it's actually needed — the
structure is already familiar and already trusted rather than
introduced for the first time under pressure.

## Common pitfalls

- **Only used after something goes wrong** — a class that never
  experiences a circle except as a response to harm treats it as
  disguised discipline rather than genuine dialogue.
- **Facilitator steers the outcome** — an adult who uses the circle to
  arrive at a predetermined consequence undermines the process's
  actual difference from a punitive response.
- **No agreement at the end** — a harm-repair circle that surfaces what
  happened but produces no concrete next step leaves the underlying
  issue unresolved.
- **Unsafe for a participant to speak honestly** — running a circle
  before there's enough trust or structure in place can pressure a
  student to share more, or less honestly, than the setting can
  actually support.
- **Confused with general [[classroom-management]]** — treating the
  circle as the classroom's whole behavior system, rather than one
  structured response within it, leaves no plan for the ordinary,
  moment-to-moment routines a classroom also needs.
