# Sales Pitch

A sales pitch is a concise narrative aimed at persuading a specific
audience to take a specific action: buy, invest, adopt, partner. It's
built to be delivered in minutes, not read at leisure — in a meeting,
an elevator, an email opener, or the first slide of a deck — and its
job is to earn the next conversation, not to close every detail of the
deal on the spot.

## Key components

- **The problem, in the audience's terms** — framed using the language
  and stakes that this specific audience actually cares about, not the
  seller's internal description of the market gap.
- **The solution** — what's being offered, stated plainly enough that
  someone hearing it for the first time understands what it actually
  is and does.
- **Proof** — evidence the solution works: a customer result, a
  demo moment, a number, a credible name. Claims without proof are just
  assertions, and audiences discount assertions.
- **The ask** — a specific, concrete request: a meeting, a trial, a
  signature, a check. A pitch that persuades but never asks for
  anything leaves the audience unsure what happens next.

## Adapting the same pitch to different audiences

The substance of a pitch — the problem, the solution, the proof — stays
constant; what changes across audiences is framing and emphasis. A pitch
to an end user leans on the day-to-day pain the product removes; the
same pitch to a budget-holder leans on cost and risk; to an investor, on
market size and trajectory. Rewriting the pitch entirely for each
audience risks losing the core narrative that's actually being tested
across all of them; changing only the opening framing and which proof
point leads keeps the pitch coherent while still speaking to what each
audience specifically cares about.

## Sales pitch vs. sales proposal

A pitch is the persuasive narrative that opens the conversation; a
[[sales-proposal]] is the detailed written document — solution, scope,
pricing, timeline — that follows once the pitch has earned enough
interest to justify the buyer's time reading it. A pitch that tries to
also carry full pricing and scope stops being a pitch and becomes a
proposal delivered badly; keep the pitch short and let the proposal do
the detailed work later.

## Common pitfalls

- **Leading with features, not the problem** — opening with what the
  product does before establishing why the audience should care leaves
  them evaluating features against a problem they haven't yet
  recognized as theirs.
- **No clear ask** — a pitch that ends on inspiration rather than a
  specific next step gets a polite "interesting," not a next meeting.
- **Identical regardless of audience** — delivering the exact same pitch
  to an engineer, a budget-holder, and an investor ignores that each is
  listening for a different payoff, and reads as not having thought
  about who's actually in the room.
- **Too long** — a pitch that takes fifteen minutes to reach the point
  has usually lost the audience well before it gets there; the whole
  format assumes brevity.
- **Proof that isn't actually proof** — vague claims ("customers love
  it") stated without a specific number, name, or result carry no more
  weight than the underlying assertion did on its own.
