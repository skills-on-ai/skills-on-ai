# Legal Trademark Registration

Trademark registration is the process of formally registering legal
protection for a brand identifier — a name, logo, slogan, or other
mark used to distinguish goods or services in commerce — one of the
main forms of intellectual property covered generally in
[[legal-intellectual-property-filing]].

## General process

1. **Clearance search** — search existing registered and unregistered
   marks to check whether the proposed mark conflicts with one already
   in use, before investing in it or filing.
2. **Determine the classes of goods/services** — trademark protection
   is registered per specific category of goods/services, not
   automatically across all commercial activity.
3. **File the application** — submit the mark, the classes, and
   evidence of use (or intent to use, in jurisdictions that allow it)
   to the relevant trademark office.
4. **Respond to office actions** — the examining office may raise
   objections (similarity to an existing mark, descriptiveness) that
   need to be addressed before registration proceeds.
5. **Maintain the registration** — most jurisdictions require periodic
   renewal and, in some cases, proof of continued use to keep the
   registration active.

## Why clearance searching matters so much

Skipping a clearance search and later discovering a conflicting
existing mark can force a costly rebrand after significant investment
in the mark (packaging, marketing, domain names) — clearance searching
before committing to a mark is far cheaper than discovering the
conflict after the fact.

## Common pitfalls

- **Skipping the clearance search** — proceeding directly to filing (or
  worse, to market) without checking for conflicting existing marks.
- **Registering in the wrong classes** — protection only covers the
  registered classes of goods/services; a mark registered in the wrong
  class offers little protection for the actual commercial use.
- **Letting registration lapse** — missing a renewal deadline or
  required proof-of-use filing can cause a registration to lapse,
  losing protection built up over years.
- **Choosing an inherently weak, purely descriptive mark** — marks that
  merely describe the product (rather than being distinctive) are
  harder to register and enforce.
