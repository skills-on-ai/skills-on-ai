# Course Syllabus Design

A syllabus is the outward-facing document that tells students and
parents what a course is, how it's graded, what's expected of them,
and roughly when each part happens. It's a communication artifact, not
an instructional plan — the actual sequencing, pacing, and assessment
design that a syllabus summarizes lives in [[curriculum-design]], and
writing a polished syllabus is no substitute for having done that
design work first.

## Key components

- **Course overview** — what the course covers and what students
  should be able to do by the end, stated in language a student or
  parent can actually understand, not internal curriculum jargon.
- **Grading policy** — how the final grade is calculated: what
  categories count, their weights, and how late work, missing work,
  and extra credit are handled, stated precisely enough to resolve a
  dispute without judgment calls later.
- **Schedule** — a unit-by-unit or week-by-week outline of what's
  covered when, detailed enough to plan around but not so rigid it
  can't absorb the ordinary slippage a real term has.
- **Classroom policies** — attendance, behavior expectations, materials
  required, communication norms (how and when the teacher can be
  reached), and any course-specific rules.
- **Support resources** — how a struggling student gets help: office
  hours, tutoring, retake or resubmission policy, and who to contact
  with questions.

## Writing for the reader, not the file

A syllabus is read by people with no access to the teacher's internal
planning: a student deciding how to prioritize their time, or a parent
trying to understand why a grade came out the way it did. Every policy
in it needs to be stated precisely enough to be enforced consistently
and to resolve a dispute later without a judgment call — "late work is
penalized" invites a different conversation with every student who
asks, where "late work loses 10% per day, up to 3 days, after which it
receives a zero" doesn't. The syllabus is often the only place a
student or parent will look before a disagreement happens, so vague
language there gets litigated in person, one student at a time,
instead of being settled once in writing.

The schedule section has a different failure mode: written with too
much precision, it becomes wrong the first week a snow day or an
assembly shifts the calendar, and a syllabus that's visibly wrong
about dates loses credibility for its policies too. Stating the
schedule at the unit or week level, with an explicit note that dates
are approximate and will be updated, keeps it both useful for planning
and durable against the ordinary disruptions a term actually has.

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague grading language** — policies phrased in a way that requires
  a judgment call every time they're applied ("late work may be
  penalized") instead of a fixed, statable rule.
- **Schedule dated too precisely** — exact calendar dates for every
  unit make the syllabus visibly wrong after the first disruption,
  rather than a week-level outline that absorbs normal slippage.
- **Curriculum jargon left in** — internal planning language (standard
  codes, pedagogical framework terms) that means nothing to the
  student or parent reading it.
- **Policies that contradict school-wide policy** — a course-level rule
  (e.g., on late work or academic integrity) that conflicts with the
  school's own stated policy creates confusion about which one
  actually governs.
- **Written once and never updated** — a syllabus that isn't revised
  when the actual course changes drifts out of sync with what students
  experience, undermining trust in the document as a reference.
