# Mood Shift Playlist

A mood shift playlist is built to carry someone from where they are to
where they want to be, by starting close to the current mood and moving
in steps. It is ordered, not shuffled. Four targets cover most needs:
calm, focus, energy, and lift.

It is a personal music tool, not a treatment for any condition.

## Input required

- **Target moods** wanted, and the usual starting moods.
- **Music the user already loves,** by artist, era, genre, or song, and
  what to avoid (a song tied to a bad memory).
- **Where it plays:** commute, desk, gym, bedtime, kitchen.
- **Lyrics or instrumental,** and any language preference.
- **Music service or library** they use, as an example only.

## Questions to ask first

Ask in **one batch of at most five questions**, skipping any already
answered:

1. Which shifts do you want (for example, stressed to calm, flat to
   energised)?
2. What songs or artists reliably work for you in each mood?
3. Are there songs or genres to avoid?
4. Where and for how long will you listen?
5. Do you want lyrics, or instrumental only, for focus?

## Method

1. **Define each shift as a pair:** from-mood and to-mood, with a length.
   Ten to thirty minutes suits many uses; treat it as a default.
2. **Start where the user is.** The first tracks should match the current
   mood, not fight it. A frantic person is rarely helped by a
   sudden slow track; begin near their pace and ease downward.
3. **Move in small steps.** Change one thing at a time across the list:
   tempo, loudness, density, or brightness. Aim for a smooth path with
   five to eight stages, then a landing track.
4. **Use the user's own anchors.** Songs they love and know are more
   reliable than a stranger's list. Mix in a few unfamiliar tracks only
   after the base is solid.
5. **Match the purpose.**
   - **Calm:** slower, softer, fewer sudden changes.
   - **Focus:** steady, low-attention, often without lyrics.
   - **Energy:** rising tempo and strong rhythm, peaking late.
   - **Lift:** start honest, then move toward warm, hopeful, familiar.
6. **Order and test.** Play it through once; mark any track that jolts, and
   move or replace it. Add a clear end so it does not run into something
   that undoes the effect.
7. **Name and place them.** Short names like "Wind down" or "Desk start".
   Put them where one tap reaches them.
8. **Pair with a cue** such as the end of work, and review monthly,
   replacing tired tracks.

## Output format

- **For each playlist:** name, from-mood, to-mood, intended length,
  setting.
- **Ordered track list** by stage (stage, track, why it fits), built from
  the user's supplied songs; if suggesting, say they are suggestions to
  check.
- **Use notes:** when to play it and when to stop.
- **Maintenance rule.**

| Stage | Track | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | User's current-mood song | Match the mood |

## Ground rules

- **Not treatment.** Music can help how a day feels. It does not treat
  depression, anxiety, or any condition.
- **Persistent low mood** (lasting weeks, or affecting sleep, appetite, or
  daily life) calls for a conversation with a doctor or mental health
  professional.
- **Thoughts of self-harm or suicide:** encourage contacting local
  emergency services or a crisis line right away, or someone trusted.
  Ask the user's country and point to the official service there; do not
  guess a number.
- **Do not invent songs.** Only name tracks the user supplied or ones the
  agent is sure exist, and say to verify them.
- **Listen safely** where hearing matters, such as driving, with
  awareness of surroundings and sound level.

## Self-check before answering

- Does each playlist start near the current mood and move gradually?
- Is the order explained stage by stage?
- Are tracks the user's own or confirmed to exist?
- Did I include the low-mood and crisis note?
- Is it clear this is not treatment?

## Common pitfalls

- **Jumping straight to the target,** which feels forced.
- **Shuffle,** which destroys the arc.
- **A sad song in a "lift" list** without a path out of it.
- **Lyrics during focus work** when the user finds them distracting.
- **Relying on music alone** for a long-running low mood.
- **Never refreshing** the list until it is unlistenable.

## Example prompt

> Build me three twenty-minute playlists from my favorites: wired to
> calm after work, flat to energised before the gym, and slumped to
> lifted on a grey morning. Order each one gradually and explain why.
