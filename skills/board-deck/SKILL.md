# Board Deck

A board deck is a presentation prepared for a company's board of
directors. It summarizes performance, surfaces risks, and lays out the
decisions the board is being asked to make at that meeting. It's a
governance document, not a general company update — an [[investor-update]]
covers similar ground but is a lighter, more frequent written piece, not
tied to a formal meeting.

## Key components

- **Key metrics up front** — the handful of numbers that actually matter
  (revenue, growth, cash runway, burn, key operational metrics) on one of
  the first slides, not buried in an appendix the board has to dig for.
- **Explicit decisions needed** — what the board is actually being asked
  to approve, weigh in on, or vote on this meeting, stated as a clear
  ask, not left implied inside a narrative update.
- **Risks and challenges, genuinely disclosed** — the real problems the
  company is facing, not softened or omitted in favor of only the good
  news; a board can't do its job without seeing what's actually going
  wrong.
- **Progress against prior commitments** — what was said at the last
  meeting, and an honest account of whether it happened, not a reset to
  a fresh narrative each time.
- **A concise appendix** — supporting detail available for board members
  who want to dig deeper, kept separate from the core narrative so it
  doesn't crowd out the few things that matter most.

## Why metrics and asks lead, not narrative

A board meeting is short and a board's job is to govern: approve
decisions, offer oversight, and provide guidance where it's needed.
Leading with a dense narrative before the numbers and the ask forces
the board to extract what it's actually there to do from a wall of
context. Leading with metrics and explicit asks respects both the
board's actual role and the limited time in the room — narrative and
supporting detail belong after the metrics and asks, not before them.

## Common pitfalls

- **Good news emphasized, real risks downplayed or omitted** — this
  undermines the board's actual ability to govern; a board that only
  hears good news can't provide oversight on the problems it never
  learns about.
- **No clear ask** — a deck full of updates but no explicit decision
  request leaves the board unsure what it's actually there to decide at
  this particular meeting.
- **Too dense with detail** — burying the few things that truly matter
  under exhaustive operational detail means the board's limited
  attention goes to the wrong things.
- **Metrics without their prior-period comparison** — a number with no
  trend context (up, down, versus plan) is hard to evaluate on its own.
- **Reused, stale template** — carrying forward a deck structure that no
  longer matches what the company or board actually needs to discuss.
