# Nonprofit Board Governance

Nonprofit board governance is the structure of roles, duties, and
committees through which a volunteer board oversees an organization it
does not manage day to day. It's a different animal from for-profit
corporate governance: board members are typically unpaid volunteers
without an equity stake, the organization has no shareholders to
answer to, and the board's core job is safeguarding a mission and
public trust rather than maximizing return on capital.

## Key components

- **Fiduciary duties** — the legal duties of care (informed,
  attentive decision-making), loyalty (acting in the organization's
  interest, not a personal one), and obedience (staying within the
  mission and applicable law) that every board member holds regardless
  of expertise or time available.
- **Board composition and terms** — a deliberate mix of skills
  (finance, legal, fundraising, program expertise) and fixed, staggered
  terms, so the board doesn't lose all institutional memory at once or
  calcify around the same members indefinitely.
- **Officer roles** — chair, vice chair, secretary, and treasurer, each
  with a specific scope of authority defined in the bylaws, not
  assumed informally.
- **Committee structure** — standing committees (finance, governance,
  fundraising) that do focused work between full board meetings and
  bring recommendations rather than every decision being made by the
  full board from scratch.
- **Executive oversight, not management** — the board hires,
  evaluates, and can remove the executive director, and sets policy,
  but does not direct day-to-day staff operations — a line that, when
  crossed, undermines the executive's ability to run the organization.

## The line between governance and management

The single most common failure mode in nonprofit boards is a board
that governs by doing the staff's job instead of overseeing it — board
members who get involved in day-to-day operational decisions, direct
individual staff members, or relitigate management calls the executive
director was hired to make. This usually comes from good intentions
(board members who care and have relevant expertise) but it erodes the
executive's authority, creates conflicting instructions for staff, and
leaves the board's actual job — strategic oversight, financial
stewardship, and holding the executive accountable to results —
undone. A board that stays at the level of policy and outcomes, and
holds the executive accountable for how those outcomes get reached,
does its job; a board that reaches past the executive to manage
individual staff or decisions has stopped doing it, no matter how much
time its members are putting in.

## Common pitfalls

- **Board members managing staff directly** — bypassing the executive
  director to direct individual employees creates conflicting
  authority and undermines the person the board itself hired to run
  the organization.
- **Rubber-stamp board** — a board that approves whatever the
  executive brings without independent review isn't exercising its
  duty of care, and offers no real check when something goes wrong.
- **No conflict-of-interest process** — a board member voting on a
  contract, vendor, or hire they have a personal stake in, with no
  disclosure process in place, breaches the duty of loyalty even when
  no harm was intended.
- **Terms that never rotate** — a board with no term limits or
  staggering either loses all institutional memory in one bad year or
  never brings in the skills and perspective the organization has since
  come to need.
- **Committees that duplicate the full board** — a committee structure
  that reviews everything the full board will review anyway adds
  meetings without adding focused expertise or faster decisions.
