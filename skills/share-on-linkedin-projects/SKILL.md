# Share on LinkedIn Projects

LinkedIn's Projects section lives on the profile itself, under
Experience/Accomplishments — it isn't a feed post, has no algorithm
deciding its reach, and isn't time-bound the way a post is. It's found
three ways: someone visits the profile directly, it surfaces in
LinkedIn's internal search (recruiters and connections filtering by
skill or keyword), or it's indexed by an external search engine on the
public profile page. That makes it evergreen, portfolio content read
by someone who already has a reason to be looking — a recruiter doing
diligence, a mutual connection checking credibility, a potential
collaborator sizing up fit — rather than a passive scroller deciding
whether to stop.

## Start

- Line 1: "<title>"
- Line 2: blank
- Line 3: "• Link: <url>"
- Line 4: "• Tags: <hashtags up to 70 characaters>"
- Line 5: "• Keywords: <comma-separated keywords up to 70 characaters>"

## Sections

- "Summary:" One paragraph like a newspaper (who, what, where, when, how, why).
- "Benefits:" Focus on the benefits for the reader.
- "Why this matters:" Focus on what it adds to the reader.
- "Please share:" Focus on how/why/where the reader can forward this project.
- "Advice welcome:" be humble, ask for help, encourage advice.
- "Associated skills:" five keywors that go in the separate web form separate Skills selector, not part of the description.

## Focus

- **Why to share.** Focus on why the reader could/should share it.
- **Examples/excerpts.** Focus on specific examples and excerpts.
- **Facts.** What's the problem that this project aims to solve.

NO:

- **No tech stack.** Do not say the tech implementation; covered elsewhere.
- **No AI callout.** Do not say AI assistance; it's covered elsewhere.
- **No language listing.** Do not say translations; it's covered elsewhere.
- **No counts.** Do not say page counts, part counts, chapter counts, etc.
- **No violence.** Do not use metaphors of war, scars, wounds, pain, etc.
- **No team section.** Do not write about a solo team, team members, etc.
- **No special formmating.** Do not bold, not italic, not bullet lists.
- **No "if".** Do not say "If you do X then Y"; instead use positive.
- **No run-on sentences.** Do not use many clauses nor too many words.
- **No passive voice.** Do not use passive, or non-subject, sentences.

Length:

- 1800-2000 characters.

## What works on LinkedIn Projects

- **It's read with intent, not scrolled past.** The reader already
  chose to look at the profile; the job of the entry is to reward that
  attention with a clear, specific account of the project, not to earn
  a stop the way a feed hook does.
- **Keywords genuinely matter here.** LinkedIn's internal search (and,
  for public profiles, external search engines) can surface a Projects
  entry to someone who's never heard of the author — using the actual
  terms a recruiter or peer would search for (a named discipline,
  tool, or methodology) does real discovery work that hashtags would
  do on other platforms.
- **Tagging team members is the real "share" lever.** Adding a
  connection as a team member notifies them and, once they confirm,
  the project appears on their profile too — this is the mechanism
  that extends an entry beyond the author's own profile, not a
  repost or boost.
- **The associated skills field is a discovery tag, not decoration.**
  Skills attached to a project feed LinkedIn's recruiter-search filters
  and skill-endorsement system — pick the two or three a search would
  actually use, not a long list.
- **A URL and media are native, not a workaround.** Unlike a feed post,
  a link here isn't penalized — attach the project's actual link plus
  a screenshot or cover image directly in the entry.
- **It's a durable landing page, not a moment.** Since nothing about it
  decays the way a feed post's reach window does, it rewards being
  genuinely complete (what it is, the author's role, the outcome)
  rather than optimized for a single day's engagement.

## Associated skills

Skills attached to a Projects entry work the way hashtags work
elsewhere: they're the tag a stranger's search actually turns up. Pick
two to five that are specific and accurate to the project (a named
discipline, technique, or tool) rather than broad ones every profile
already lists — a specific skill is both more credible and more likely
to match a recruiter's or collaborator's actual filter. Skills that
don't genuinely apply dilute the entry's credibility without adding
reach.

## Structure for a description up to 2000 characters

Even with no truncation-driven feed hook to write around, most
profile viewers skim before committing to read fully — the discipline
of front-loading the point still matters, just for a different reason.

1. **A clear, specific project title.** Not a slogan — the actual name
   a search or a skimming recruiter would recognize.
2. **First ~140 characters — what it is and why it might matter**,
   stated plainly enough to work for a stranger with zero prior
   context, since this is what a search result or notification preview
   shows.
3. **One honest, personal line.** Why it exists, what it took, or
   what's still uncertain — the detail that reads as a person
   describing real work, not a resume bullet.
4. **The author's actual role and what the project involved**, in
   plain terms — this is what a recruiter or collaborator is actually
   evaluating.
5. **One specific ask.** A kind of feedback wanted, a use case not yet
   considered, or an invitation to reach out — something a reader can
   act on by messaging directly, since there's no comment thread here
   to invite replies into.
6. **The link and a screenshot or cover image**, attached natively.
7. **Team members tagged**, for any collaborator who's also on
   LinkedIn and consents to being associated with the project.

## Key components

- **Specific, searchable language** — the terms a recruiter or peer
  would type, used naturally in the description, not stuffed in.
- **Humility over resume-speak** — "here's what I built and what I'm
  still refining" reads as more credible to someone doing diligence
  than achievement-only phrasing.
- **A message-able ask** — phrased for someone to act on by reaching
  out directly, since this format has no comment thread to invite
  replies into.
- **Team members tagged deliberately**, as the entry's actual
  distribution mechanism, not an afterthought.
- **Pairing with a feed post** — the Projects entry is the durable
  landing page; a [[share-on-linkedin]] post announcing it is what
  actually drives people to visit the profile and find it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating it like a feed post** — writing for a scroll-stopping hook
  when the actual reader already chose to look, and needs substance
  more than a hook.
- **Vague, resume-bullet phrasing** ("led a project to improve
  outcomes") with none of the specific, searchable detail that makes
  the entry findable or credible.
- **Skipping the associated skills field**, losing the entry's main
  path into recruiter and peer search.
- **Forgetting to tag team members**, missing the one mechanism this
  format has for reaching beyond the author's own profile visitors.
- **No link or media attached**, leaving a reader with no way to
  actually go see the project.
- **Publishing the entry alone**, with no companion feed post pointing
  people to it — an entry no one is told about mostly sits unread.
