# Prospect theory

Prospect theory, developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky,
describes how people really choose under risk, as opposed to how
expected-value theory says they should. Its core findings: people judge
outcomes as gains or losses from a *reference point*, not as final
wealth; losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains please
(*loss aversion*); sensitivity diminishes as amounts grow; small
probabilities are overweighted and moderate-to-large ones underweighted;
and the same choice framed differently produces different decisions.
Using it means spotting these patterns in a decision, whether yours or
someone else's, and correcting or designing for them.

## Input required

- **The choice** and the options, with outcomes and probabilities.
- **The reference point** the decision-maker is using (status quo,
  expectation, sunk cost, a peer's result).
- **How the options are framed** — as gains or losses, in what
  wording.
- **Who is deciding and for whom** — oneself, a team, customers.

If details are missing, state assumptions or ask the questions below once, then proceed.

## Questions to ask first

Ask in **one batch of at most five questions**, skipping any already
answered, then proceed:

1. What are the options, with outcomes and probabilities?
2. What does the decider treat as "normal" or "break-even" right now?
3. Is any option described as avoiding a loss versus gaining a benefit?
4. Is there a sunk cost, a deadline, or a recent loss influencing the
   choice?
5. Is the aim to improve your own decision or to design an offer or
   message for others?

## The core ideas

- **Reference dependence** — outcomes are coded as gains or losses
  relative to a reference point; moving the reference point changes
  the choice.
- **Loss aversion** — a loss looms larger than an equal gain, by
  about 2:1 in many studies.
- **Diminishing sensitivity** — the difference between $0 and $100
  feels larger than between $1,000 and $1,100.
- **Risk attitudes flip** — typically *risk-averse for gains*
  (take the sure $500 over a 50% shot at $1,000) and *risk-seeking
  for losses* (gamble to avoid a sure loss).
- **Probability weighting** — tiny chances are overweighted (lottery
  tickets, insurance for rare events); near-certainty is
  underweighted; the *certainty effect* makes a sure thing special.
- **Framing** — "90% survival" versus "10% mortality" gives the same
  facts but different choices.
- **Related effects** — endowment effect (valuing what one owns
  more), sunk-cost behavior (throwing good money after bad), status quo
  bias, and mental accounting.

## Method

1. **Restate the decision in neutral terms**: final outcomes and
   probabilities, no gain/loss language.
2. **Identify the reference point** being used and which outcomes
   feel like gains and which like losses.
3. **Find the distortions.** Check for loss aversion keeping the status
   quo, risk-seeking to dodge a sure loss, overweighted rare events,
   and sunk costs steering the choice.
4. **Re-frame several ways.** Evaluate the options with different
   reference points (current state, goal, outside view) and as final
   totals. If the preference flips, the framing is driving it.
5. **Compare with the rational benchmark.** Compute the
   [[expected-value-analysis]] and ask whether the preferred option
   differs, and whether the difference is justified by real risk
   limits, not just feeling.
6. **Decide on purpose.** Keep the intuition if it reflects real
   values (a ruinous loss deserves extra weight); correct it if it
   reflects framing.
7. **For designing offers or communications**, apply the findings
   honestly: present information clearly, make defaults sensible, and
   avoid exploiting biases against the audience's interests.

## Output format

- **Neutral restatement** of the choice.
- **Reference point and frame** currently in play.
- **Distortions detected**, each with how it shows up.
- **Reframed comparison** — does the preference change?
- **Benchmark** — expected value and risk, versus the intuitive choice.
- **Recommendation** — what to do, and the reasoning that survives
  reframing.

## Ground rules

- **Describe, don't scold.** These patterns are normal human
  psychology, not stupidity.
- **Not every deviation is an error.** Loss aversion can be rational
  when a loss threatens survival.
- **Effects vary.** The 2:1 ratio is an average from experiments;
  individuals, stakes, and cultures differ.
- **Don't manipulate.** Use the theory to inform and to remove
  distortions, not to push people into choices against their interests.

## Self-check before answering

- Was the choice restated without gain/loss framing?
- Were reference point, loss aversion, probability weighting, and sunk
  costs each considered?
- Was the preference tested under at least one alternative frame?
- Is the advice separate from the description of the bias?

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating the status quo as risk-free.** Doing nothing has costs.
- **Sunk-cost chasing** — continuing because of past investment.
- **Overweighting dramatic, rare events** — and neglecting common
  ones.
- **Gambling to avoid a sure loss** — turning a small loss into a large
  one.
- **Framing blindness** — believing the wording did not matter.
- **Overgeneralizing the research** — applying lab averages to a
  specific person as if they were laws.

## Example prompt

> We've spent $400k on a project that's behind schedule. Management
> says "we can't stop now." Use prospect theory to explain what biases
> may be at work, re-frame the choice, and tell me what the decision
> looks like with sunk costs ignored.
