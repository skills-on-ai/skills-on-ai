# Property Management

Property management is the ongoing operational work of running a
rental property or portfolio on behalf of an owner, across the whole
lifecycle of ownership rather than any single tenancy. Where a
[[lease-agreement]] sets the terms with one tenant, property management
is everything that happens around and between leases — collecting
rent, handling maintenance, communicating with tenants, and keeping
the property in good condition — often across many units and tenants
over time.

## Key components

- **Rent collection and arrears handling** — a defined process for how
  rent is collected, what happens the moment a payment is late, and a
  consistent escalation path (reminder, late fee, formal notice) rather
  than an ad hoc response that varies tenant to tenant.
- **Maintenance request process with a realistic response-time
  expectation** — a clear way for tenants to submit requests, and a
  stated target for how quickly each priority level (emergency,
  urgent, routine) gets acknowledged and resolved.
- **Tenant communication and turnover process between leases** — how
  tenants are kept informed during the tenancy, and a standard sequence
  for what happens between one tenant moving out and the next moving
  in: notice, move-out inspection, repairs, re-listing, and move-in.
- **Routine property inspections** — scheduled checks of the property's
  condition during the tenancy, not only at move-in and move-out,
  catching maintenance issues before they become expensive.
- **Financial reporting to the owner** — income, expenses, and
  outstanding issues reported to the owner on a regular cadence they
  can actually act on.

## Why a defined maintenance response process matters for tenant retention

Slow or unclear handling of maintenance requests is consistently one
of the most common tenant complaints, and a real driver of tenants
choosing not to renew. A tenant who doesn't know whether a "someone
will look at it" request means tomorrow or next month has no way to
plan around it, and experiences the delay as being ignored even if
work is actually in progress. A stated response-time target, tracked
and met, does two things at once: it gives tenants a concrete
expectation instead of uncertainty, and it gives management a way to
measure its own performance instead of relying on impressions.

## Common pitfalls

- **Maintenance requests with no tracked response-time expectation** —
  tenants have no idea what to expect, and management has no way to
  measure or improve its own responsiveness without a target to
  compare against.
- **Turnover handled reactively with no standard process** — each
  vacancy is worked out from scratch instead of following a repeatable
  sequence, so units sit empty longer than necessary and repairs get
  missed or duplicated.
- **Inconsistent records of condition and repair history** — without a
  documented baseline and repair log per unit, a later dispute over
  damage, a deposit, or a maintenance failure has nothing agreed to
  point to.
- **No escalation path for arrears** — inconsistent, personality-driven
  responses to late rent instead of a process applied the same way
  every time.
- **Owner reporting that's irregular or after the fact** — an owner who
  only hears about a problem once it's already serious has lost the
  chance to weigh in earlier.
