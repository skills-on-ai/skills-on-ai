# Flexibility and Mobility Fitness

Flexibility and mobility training improves a joint's range of motion
and the ease of moving through that range — distinct but related
goals: flexibility refers to a muscle's ability to lengthen, while
mobility refers to a joint's ability to move actively and under
control through its range.

## Static vs. dynamic stretching

- **Static stretching** — holding a stretch at a fixed position for an
  extended time; generally better suited after exercise or as a
  standalone flexibility practice, since some research suggests it can
  temporarily reduce power output if done immediately before intense
  activity.
- **Dynamic stretching** — moving through a range of motion actively
  and repeatedly (leg swings, arm circles); generally the better choice
  as a warm-up before training, since it also raises body temperature
  and rehearses movement patterns relevant to the activity ahead.

## Building a mobility routine

Effective mobility work typically targets the joints most limited by
daily habits — for many people, this includes hips and thoracic spine
(upper/mid back), which are heavily affected by prolonged sitting.
Consistency matters far more than session length: short, regular
mobility work produces better long-term results than infrequent, long
sessions.

## Common pitfalls

- **Static stretching as a warm-up before intense activity** — dynamic
  movement generally prepares the body better for immediate
  performance; static stretching is better placed after training or as
  its own separate practice.
- **Bouncing/forcing a stretch** — ballistic, forceful stretching
  increases injury risk compared to controlled, gradual stretching.
- **Inconsistent practice** — mobility and flexibility gains fade
  without regular practice; sporadic effort produces correspondingly
  limited and temporary results.
- **Ignoring pain signals** — stretching into sharp pain (rather than
  mild discomfort) risks injury; flexibility should improve gradually,
  not be forced.
