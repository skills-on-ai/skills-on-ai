# Lesson Plan

A lesson plan is a structured plan for one teaching session: what
learners should be able to do by the end, how the session opens, what
the main instructional activity is, how the teacher will know before
the session ends whether it landed, and what materials it needs. It's
scoped to a single session, not the sequence of sessions a course is
made of.

## Key components

- **Learning objective** — a specific statement of what learners should
  be able to do by the end of this session, written so it's actually
  checkable. See [[smart-criteria]] for making it measurable rather
  than aspirational.
- **Opening hook** — a short activity, question, or demonstration that
  captures attention and connects to what learners already know before
  new material is introduced.
- **Main instructional activity** — the core teaching method for the
  session: direct instruction, guided practice, discussion, lab, or
  some combination, sized to the time actually available.
- **Check for understanding** — a way to confirm before the session
  ends whether the objective was met: a quick quiz, exit ticket, cold
  call, or short demonstration from learners themselves.
- **Materials** — everything the session needs to run: handouts,
  slides, equipment, software, or physical supplies, listed so nothing
  is discovered missing mid-session.
- **Timing** — an estimated duration for each part, so the plan fits
  the actual class period rather than an idealized one.

## Why the closing check matters

A session can feel like it went well and still have missed its
objective entirely — a teacher standing at the front has no reliable
signal for what actually landed in learners' heads unless they build
in a specific check before the session ends. Without one, the first
sign that the objective wasn't met often arrives on a later test or
assignment, far too late to reteach the material while it's still
fresh and relevant. The check doesn't need to be elaborate: a single
well-chosen question that only makes sense if the objective was
achieved is enough to catch a session that didn't land.

## Sizing the plan to the time

A lesson plan is only useful if it's realistic about how much a single
session can cover. Estimate time for each part before the session, not
after, and cut content rather than rushing the check for understanding
when the estimate runs long — a session that covers less material but
confirms it landed is more useful than one that covers everything on
the list but leaves no time to check any of it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague objective** — "understand fractions" gives no way to tell,
  at the end of the session, whether it actually happened; a checkable
  objective names what learners can *do* ("add two fractions with
  different denominators"), not what they'll vaguely "understand."
- **Overpacked plan** — more content than the session's actual time
  allows, so the check for understanding (or the practice that leads to
  it) is the first thing cut when time runs short.
- **No check for understanding** — a session that didn't land goes
  unnoticed until a later test or assignment, when reteaching is much
  more costly than catching it the same day.
- **Hook disconnected from the objective** — an engaging opening that
  doesn't actually connect to what the session is trying to teach
  wastes the attention it captures.
- **Materials assumed rather than listed** — discovering mid-session
  that a handout wasn't printed or a tool wasn't installed derails the
  timing for everything after it.
