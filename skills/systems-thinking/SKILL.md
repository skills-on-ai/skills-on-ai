# Systems Thinking

Systems thinking is a discipline for understanding how a set of
interconnected parts behaves as a whole — recognizing that a system's
behavior emerges from the *relationships and feedback* between its
components, not from any single component examined in isolation. It is
the foundational thinking style that specific methods like
[[vanguard-method]] and [[causal-analysis-based-on-system-theory]]
apply to particular domains (service management, accident investigation).

## Core concepts

- **Stocks and flows** — a stock is an accumulation (inventory, backlog
  size, team morale); flows are the rates that increase or decrease it
  (arrivals, completions). Most "why is this number wrong" questions are
  really questions about an imbalanced flow, not the stock itself.
- **Feedback loops** — a **reinforcing loop** amplifies change in one
  direction (more sales → more marketing budget → more sales); a
  **balancing loop** pushes back toward a goal or equilibrium (backlog
  grows → team pressure increases → more focus → backlog shrinks).
  Most persistent organizational problems are sustained by a loop, not a
  single cause.
- **Delays** — the gap between an action and its visible effect. A delay
  between cause and effect is what makes people overcorrect (increasing
  a fix's dose because the first dose "didn't work yet") and is behind
  many systems' oscillating, overshoot-and-collapse behavior.
- **Leverage points** — Donella Meadows' insight that some places to
  intervene in a system produce far more change than others, and the
  most obvious intervention point (a number, a target) is usually the
  *weakest* lever; deeper leverage lies in the rules, the information
  flows, and the system's underlying purpose/goal.

## Systems thinking vs. linear/reductionist analysis

Linear analysis breaks a problem into independent parts, fixes each part,
and assumes the whole improves proportionally. This works well for
genuinely decomposable problems, but fails when parts interact: fixing
one team's local metric can make the overall system worse if it shifts
the bottleneck or breaks a feedback loop other parts depended on. Systems
thinking asks "what does this change do to the *whole* system's
behavior over time," not just "does this specific part now measure
better."

## A basic working method

1. **Define the boundary** — decide what's inside the system being
   studied and what's an external input, since including too little
   misses the real interactions and including too much makes the
   analysis unmanageable.
2. **Map the structure** — identify the key stocks, flows, and feedback
   loops (a causal loop diagram is the standard visual tool), rather than
   listing isolated causes.
3. **Look for the archetype** — many recurring problems fit known
   patterns ("fixes that fail," "shifting the burden," "tragedy of the
   commons"); recognizing the pattern often reveals the leverage point
   faster than re-deriving the structure from scratch.
4. **Intervene at structure, not symptom** — change the rules, incentives,
   or information flows that produce the unwanted behavior, rather than
   repeatedly treating the symptom the structure keeps regenerating.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating a systemic problem as a people problem** — when a system's
  structure consistently produces a bad outcome, replacing the people in
  it usually just reproduces the same outcome with different people.
- **Optimizing one part in isolation** — a local optimization that
  ignores the part's effect on the rest of the system can move the
  bottleneck elsewhere or break a loop other parts relied on, leaving the
  whole system worse off even as the optimized part's own metric improves.
- **Confusing correlation in a snapshot for the system's actual causal
  structure** — a system with feedback and delays can show a misleading
  correlation at any single point in time; understanding the loops and
  delays matters more than a one-time data snapshot.
- **Picking the most visible, lowest-leverage intervention** — changing a
  number or a target is usually the weakest lever available; the
  underlying rules and incentives that produced the number are usually
  the stronger, if less obvious, intervention point.
