# Italian Language

Italiano (Italian) is a Romance language, official in Italy, Switzerland
(one of four national languages), San Marino, and Vatican City. It's
written in the Latin alphabet with a small set of accented letters
(à, è, é, ì, ò, ù) marking stress on certain word-final syllables, and
doubled consonants (nn, tt, ll) that change both pronunciation and
meaning (pena "sorrow" vs. penna "pen") — dropping a doubled letter is a
real spelling error, not a cosmetic one.

## Phonetic spelling

Italian spelling maps to pronunciation more consistently than English or
French: most letters and letter combinations (gli, gn, sc before e/i,
ch, gh) correspond to predictable sounds once the rules are known, and
there are few silent letters. This makes Italian relatively
straightforward to read aloud correctly from text, but text-processing
tools still need to preserve accents and doubled consonants precisely,
since both carry real meaning distinctions.

## Grammatical gender

Nouns are masculine or feminine, and articles and adjectives agree:
il ragazzo alto (the tall boy), la ragazza alta (the tall girl). Gender
is fairly predictable from the ending (-o typically masculine, -a
typically feminine) but has exceptions (la mano is feminine despite the
-o-like sound, il problema is masculine despite ending in -a). Plural
endings also shift with gender and often with the final vowel (ragazzo
→ ragazzi, ragazza → ragazze), which differs from the simpler
add-an-s pattern of English.

## Formal/informal address: tu and Lei

Italian distinguishes informal tu from formal Lei for singular "you."
Tu is used with family, friends, children, and peers; Lei is used with
strangers, elders, and in professional or first-contact contexts. The
distinctive and often surprising feature: Lei grammatically takes
third-person singular verb forms and third-person pronouns/possessives,
as though addressing a third party ("Lei viene?" — literally "does she
come?" — used as the polite way to ask "are you coming?" to the person
directly in front of you). Formal Lei is often capitalized in careful
writing to distinguish it from the ordinary third-person "lei" (she).

## Regional dialects and standard Italian

Italy has many regional language varieties — Sicilian, Neapolitan,
Venetian, and others — that in some cases are distinct enough from
standard Italian to be classified by linguists as separate languages
rather than dialects of Italian, despite common Italian usage calling
them "dialetti." Standard Italian, based historically on the Florentine/
Tuscan variety, is what's used in formal writing, education, media, and
virtually all professional or official text — regional forms appear
mainly in informal speech, local literature, or deliberate stylistic
choices, not in standard written communication.

## Common pitfalls

- **Dropping doubled consonants** (penna → pena, palla → pala) — this
  changes the word's meaning, not just its spelling weight.
- **Using tu where Lei is expected** in formal or first-contact written
  communication, which can read as overly familiar or disrespectful.
- **Conjugating Lei with second-person verb forms** instead of
  third-person — a common error for those used to languages where the
  formal pronoun just swaps in with the same verb pattern.
- **Guessing gender purely from the final letter** without checking
  known exceptions like il problema, il giorno's irregular plural, or
  la mano.
- **Treating a regional dialect (Sicilian, Neapolitan) as equivalent
  to standard Italian** in formal or general-audience writing.
