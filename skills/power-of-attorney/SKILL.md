# Power of Attorney

A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document in which one person (the
principal) authorizes another person (the agent, sometimes called the
attorney-in-fact) to act on their behalf — signing documents, managing
finances, or making medical decisions — within whatever scope the
document grants. Its job is to let someone else legally step into the
principal's shoes when the principal can't act, or chooses not to act,
for themselves.

## Key components

- **Scope** — exactly what the agent is authorized to do: everything
  from a narrow, single-purpose grant (sell one specific property) to
  broad general authority over all financial and legal affairs. The
  document should state what the agent is *not* authorized to do just
  as clearly as what they are.
- **Durable vs. non-durable** — a durable power of attorney remains
  valid if the principal becomes incapacitated; a non-durable one
  automatically ends at that point, which defeats the purpose for
  anyone drafting a POA specifically to cover incapacity.
- **Effective date** — when the authority actually starts: immediately
  upon signing, or "springing," meaning it only takes effect once a
  specified condition is met (typically a doctor's determination that
  the principal is incapacitated). A springing POA needs a clear,
  workable process for proving the triggering condition has happened,
  or the agent can't use it when it's actually needed.
- **Revocation** — how and when the principal can cancel the POA while
  still competent to do so, typically in writing and communicated to
  the agent and any institutions relying on it. A POA automatically
  ends on the principal's death, at which point the executor named in
  their will takes over instead.

## Why the choice of agent matters

A power of attorney can grant sweeping authority over someone's money,
property, or medical care, often with little day-to-day oversight once
it's signed. The agent doesn't need to post a bond, doesn't need
court approval for routine decisions, and may be the only person
watching how the authority gets used. Choosing an agent is really
choosing who has effectively unsupervised control over some part of
the principal's life — trustworthiness and willingness to act in the
principal's interest matter far more here than convenience or default
assumptions about who "should" be asked (an eldest child, a spouse).

## Common pitfalls

- **Scope so broad the principal doesn't realize what they've
  authorized** — a general, immediately effective POA can let the
  agent act right away, on nearly anything, without the principal ever
  intending day-to-day control to change hands.
- **No plan for the named agent becoming unable or unwilling to
  serve** — no alternate agent named, so if the first choice dies,
  becomes incapacitated themselves, or simply won't act, there's no
  authorized agent left and a court may need to appoint one.
- **Confusing financial and medical/healthcare power of attorney as
  the same document** — they typically are not. A financial POA
  covers money and property; a medical or healthcare POA (sometimes
  called a health care proxy) covers medical decisions. Someone may
  want the same person, or different people, for each, but assuming
  one document covers both is a common and consequential mistake.
- **Springing POA with no clear trigger process** — "upon
  incapacity" with no defined way to establish that incapacity (which
  doctor, what standard, what document) can leave an agent unable to
  act at the exact moment they're needed.
- **Institutions refusing to honor an old or unfamiliar POA** — banks
  and other institutions sometimes balk at older documents or ones
  that don't match their internal forms, which is worth flagging as a
  real-world friction point even when the document is otherwise valid.

## A note on legal advice

This is general guidance on what a power of attorney typically covers
and why, not legal advice. Requirements for validity, the difference
between durable and non-durable authority, and how financial and
medical POAs are structured vary by jurisdiction — always direct the
reader to have their POA drafted or reviewed by a qualified lawyer
licensed in their jurisdiction.
