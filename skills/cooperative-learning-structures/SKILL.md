# Cooperative Learning Structures

Cooperative learning structures are specific, repeatable formats for
organizing peer or small-group work — think-pair-share, jigsaw,
numbered heads together, round robin — chosen deliberately for what
they make possible instructionally, rather than "group work" used as a
generic label for putting students in clusters and hoping
collaboration happens on its own.

## Key components

- **A named structure matched to the goal** — think-pair-share for
  quick individual processing before sharing, jigsaw for dividing
  content across group members who then teach each other, numbered
  heads together for ensuring any group member can answer for the
  group — chosen for what the lesson actually needs, not picked at
  random.
- **Individual accountability** — a mechanism (a cold call, a written
  product, a randomly-selected reporter) that makes each student's own
  understanding visible, not just the group's collective output.
- **Positive interdependence** — a task designed so the group actually
  needs each member's contribution to succeed, rather than one that
  can be completed by a single capable student while others watch.
- **Clear roles or steps** — an explicit sequence (think alone, pair,
  share) or assigned roles, so the structure doesn't collapse into
  unstructured talk the moment students start working.
- **Time limits per stage** — a bounded amount of time for each step of
  the structure, since an open-ended "discuss with your group" tends
  to expand to fill the period without producing more learning.

## Why the structure matters more than the grouping

Simply putting students in groups and assigning a task doesn't
reliably produce cooperative learning — it reliably produces one of a
few common failure modes: one student does the work while others
disengage, the loudest voice dominates the group's output, or students
socialize instead of working, because nothing in an unstructured group
task requires any individual to actually engage. A named cooperative
structure solves this by building the accountability and
interdependence into the format itself rather than depending on
students to self-organize well.

This is why the specific structure matters, not just the fact of
grouping. Think-pair-share works because the individual "think" step
happens before any social pressure to conform to a partner's answer
exists. Jigsaw works because each student holds a piece of content no
one else in the group has, making their contribution genuinely
necessary rather than optional. Numbered heads together works because
no student knows in advance whether they'll be the one called on to
answer for the group, which keeps every member engaged with the
group's actual answer rather than deferring to whoever seems most
confident. Picking the wrong structure for the goal — jigsaw for
content that doesn't divide cleanly, think-pair-share for a task that
needs more than two people — undermines the same mechanism that makes
the right structure work.

## Common pitfalls

- **"Group work" with no named structure** — an open-ended group task
  with no defined roles or steps tends to reproduce the same
  engagement problems unstructured grouping was meant to solve.
- **No individual accountability** — a task that can be completed and
  reported by one group member leaves the rest of the group free to
  disengage without consequence.
- **Structure mismatched to the task** — using a structure built for
  one kind of goal (quick individual processing) for a task that needs
  another (deep collaborative problem-solving) undercuts the mechanism
  that makes the structure work.
- **No time limit on a stage** — an unbounded discussion step tends to
  expand past its useful point and crowd out the rest of the lesson.
- **Grouping treated as the whole plan** — assuming that arranging
  desks into groups is itself the instructional design, rather than
  the structure and accountability layered on top of that arrangement.
